Mass properties — the mass, center of mass, and moments of inertia of the spacecraft — are engineering parameters essential for mission success. They must fall within a specified range so the launch vehicle can deliver the spacecraft to its target orbit and so the spacecraft attitude control system can point the spacecraft accurately enough to meet science requirements. They also demonstrate that the structure will survive the loads it will experience during launch. Each must be tracked continuously throughout the I&T program, measured precisely at the integrated spacecraft level, and delivered to the launch authority as launch inputs. An error in the mass properties database is not a paperwork problem — it is a mission risk, and not always an abstract one: in 2016, a Japanese X-ray observatory came apart in orbit within weeks of launch after its attitude control system was updated with the wrong mass properties for its own deployed configuration.

"Mass is tallied at nearly every level of assembly — down to the fastener, when mass is critical — so it is easy to track and hard to lose control of. Center of mass and moments of inertia are different. Until the instrument, payload, or full spacecraft comes together, they are largely estimates and design goals, not measured quantities. The actual value typically only show up at a handful of major assembly milestones. By then the value largely is what it is. It's not that CoM and MOI matter less than mass. It's that they're measured far less often, and the attitude control system still needs to know them accurately."

This article is an introduction to spacecraft mass properties and structural loads verification — how mass, center of mass, and moments of inertia are measured and tracked through the program, how the launch load environment is characterized, and how the finite element model is correlated and closed against those loads through coupled loads analysis. 1. Why Mass Properties Matter from Day One

The spacecraft's mass is not a single number — it is a collection of numbers, each defined for a specific mission phase, each with a different impact on a different aspect of the mission. The launch mass includes the spacecraft bus, all instruments, all propellant, all pressurant, and all ordnance — everything that leaves the ground. This projected number, together with adequate margin, is what determines which launch vehicles are even viable candidates for the mission in the first place — a spacecraft can't be sized against a vehicle that hasn't been narrowed down yet. Once a vehicle is actually selected, launch mass becomes the number that has to be confirmed against that vehicle's advertised performance to that orbit, with margin intact. The beginning-of-life (BOL) mass is the spacecraft after some propellant is expended but before mission operations begin, and it determines the attitude control authority available at the start of the mission operations phase. The end-of-life (EOL) mass — the spacecraft's mass once its operational mission is complete, whether that means propellant fully depleted or a disposal reserve set aside specifically for a deorbit or end-of-mission maneuver — determines the final orbital parameters of the spacecraft and whether any deorbit or disposal requirement can actually be met.

Each of these numbers has its own budget established for every stage of the build, and until real hardware exists, that budgeted value is what the spacecraft's analytical models — including the FEM discussed later — actually use. As components, subsystems, and eventually the integrated spacecraft get built, mass is tallied along the way, sometimes down to the fastener level when mass is genuinely critical, and tracked continuously against the budget. Every design change that affects mass has to be captured in the mass properties database as it happens; the mechanical systems team maintains this database and produces a current mass roll-up at every major program review. Those measured, as-built numbers replace the original budgeted assumptions one level at a time, until the fully integrated spacecraft is finally weighed and the model is verified against real, measured mass.

3. Mass Measurement — Methods and Uncertainty

At the component level, mass measurement is straightforward: a precision scale calibrated to the required accuracy, used in a controlled environment. Every scale — whether in the I&T facility or at a supplier's site — must carry a current calibration traceable to a national standard, so measurements from different instruments and organizations all carry the same basis and can be trusted and compared. The real challenge is bookkeeping: every component must be weighed and entered into the mass properties database with its part number, serial number, and measured date. That consistency is what makes the mass budget worth tracking — a subsystem approaching its allocation can, with review, draw unused margin from another running light, keeping the spacecraft within its total allowance. For the I&T lead, this makes the mass properties report reviewed at each program milestone worth real attention: it's the earliest warning of a margin problem serious enough to eventually affect schedule, staffing, or facility planning.

At the spacecraft level, the measurement is more complex — the spacecraft is too large, fragile, and valuable to place on an unsupported platform. Instead it's supported on a purpose-built weighing fixture with calibrated load cells at three or more defined support points, depending on the spacecraft's geometry and available mounting locations. Designing and characterizing that fixture is the mechanical team's responsibility, not I&T's; what matters to the I&T lead is that this is a real facility event with its own setup time and fixture, distinct from the spacecraft's day-to-day handling fixture, and it needs adequate schedule and resources like any other measurement in this article (§9). A three-point fixture is simplest: geometry alone fully determines how load splits between the legs, even if the split is uneven. Four-or-more-point fixtures offer more stability for large or awkward spacecraft, but the load split can no longer be predicted by geometry alone — a fixture that isn't properly characterized for this can quietly misreport how load is shared among its legs, which is a risk well-designed fixtures build in compliance specifically to prevent.

Measurement uncertainty comes from several sources: load cell calibration accuracy, tare accuracy, and temperature stability. Load cells drift with temperature even when compensated, so the environment needs to be held stable — and hardware moved in from a different thermal environment needs time to acclimate, since it stirs up local air currents that can disturb a sensitive reading. For spacecraft-level mass measurement, the achievable result is typically on the order of ±0.1% of the total mass (see §11) — for a multi-thousand-kilogram spacecraft, that can still mean several kilograms of uncertainty the launch vehicle performance calculation and propellant budget must absorb.

All of this discipline exists to protect the launch vehicle's mass-to-orbit capability. Once a spacecraft is committed to a vehicle, every kilogram saved on the dry spacecraft becomes a kilogram of propellant margin, delta-v, or mission life. Industry mass growth allowances typically run from around 5% for heritage hardware up to 30% or more for new designs, and propellant budgets commonly carry a comparable margin of their own. These margins don't guarantee an overrun won't happen — even JWST exceeded its target launch mass by more than 12% during development, a discrepancy that took roughly thirteen months and nearly a billion dollars to resolve. The practical lesson for the I&T lead is vigilance: watching the mass growth trend at successive reviews, not just the latest snapshot, is what surfaces this risk while there's still schedule and margin to respond to it.

Center of mass and moments of inertia, covered next, are rarely known with real certainty until late in assembly — which is exactly why so many spacecraft carry mass trim provisions from the start: ground-installed balance weights correcting for build tolerances, gravity sag between the 1-G assembly environment and on-orbit zero-G, and balancing-process uncertainty. Ballast is not free mass — it draws from the same budget as everything else, and if the dry mass allocation has already consumed most of its growth margin by the time trim mass is identified as necessary, ballast competes directly with the propellant budget for whatever allowance remains. It's worth confirming early, not late, whether ballast has actually been accounted for in the mass budget.

Mass growth allowance and margin figures follow industry-standard mass properties control practice. The JWST example is drawn from public program review findings.

◆ Case Study — James Webb Space Telescope, 2012

A 2012 Government Accountability Office review found that JWST's spacecraft element had exceeded its target launch mass by more than 12% during development, triggering a thirteen-month mass-reduction effort that added roughly two years and nearly a billion dollars to the program. 4. Center of Mass and Its Consequences

The center of mass (CoM) — the point about which the spacecraft's mass is evenly distributed in all directions — is the reference point for all attitude dynamics calculations. The attitude control system applies torques about the spacecraft's actual center of mass. If the CoM is not at the location the attitude control software assumes, the relationship between applied torque and resulting angular acceleration is incorrect, and the pointing accuracy and stability of the spacecraft will be degraded. This isn't only a concern once the spacecraft reaches its operational configuration. Any propulsive maneuver executed on the way there — a trajectory correction, an orbit insertion burn — depends on the same accurate CoM knowledge, and an error there risks the spacecraft not reaching its intended destination correctly in the first place, before pointing, stability, or station-keeping ever become the operative concern.

CoM measurement at the spacecraft level is performed by measuring the spacecraft's mass at known support points, then shifting the points and remeasuring — the change in load distribution, combined with known geometry, gives the CoM location in three axes. The precision required, typically a few millimeters for a science spacecraft, feeds directly into attitude control performance, so it's worth confirming this precision target is specified and agreed before the measurement. CoM measurement also carries the same thermal sensitivity as mass measurement, and more of it, since the calculation depends on support-point geometry as well as load distribution — the same discipline applies with less margin for error: confirm thermal soak time has been met before the measurement, not after.

CoM measurement is where an under-characterized four-or-more-point fixture creates its most consequential risk, and the failure is silent. A properly characterized fixture is calibrated against known test masses at known, offset locations specifically so the calculation doesn't rely on geometry alone — and that characterization can go stale if a bolt loosens or a mount wears without anyone re-verifying it. A second, separate risk is timing: a CoM measurement can be accurate at the moment it's taken and still be wrong by launch if something changes the mass distribution afterward — a late harness rework, a component swap, ballast added after the fact — without the program recognizing the earlier value needs to be reverified. Either way, nothing announces the error; it can sit unnoticed until after separation, when the attitude control system tries to stabilize using a CoM value that was wrong all along. Confirming fixture characterization is the mechanical team's job, but flagging a late configuration change that could affect CoM — and asking whether it warrants remeasurement — is exactly the kind of gap an I&T lead is positioned to catch, since I&T often coordinates that late-stage rework itself.

◆ The Propellant Mass CoM Shift

One of the most frequently underestimated mass properties effects is the shift in center of mass as propellant is consumed during the mission, since propellant is distributed in specific tank locations that may not coincide with the spacecraft's overall CoM. This shift must be explicitly modeled in the attitude control system's performance analysis for every mission phase — a bipropellant system drawing at a fixed mixture ratio shifts CoM predictably, while a system with multiple tanks and thruster clusters used at different mission phases may need explicit propellant management planning to keep CoM within the ACS operating envelope throughout the mission.

5. Moments of Inertia and Attitude Control

The moments of inertia — the rotational equivalents of mass, representing the resistance of the spacecraft to angular acceleration about each of its three principal axes — are as important to attitude control performance as the CoM location. The attitude control system is designed to produce a specified pointing accuracy using reaction wheels, thrusters, or control moment gyros sized to produce torques adequate for the spacecraft's expected moment of inertia. If the actual moments differ significantly from the design values, the control system performance will be degraded — and as with CoM, this isn't only a concern once the spacecraft reaches its operational configuration. A trajectory correction or orbit insertion burn still depends on the attitude control system holding the spacecraft's pointing correctly throughout the maneuver, using actuators sized against its expected moment of inertia; an error there risks the maneuver itself, not just pointing performance afterward. This is exactly why a measured MOI that doesn't match what the ACS team designed against — covered later in this section — is worth flagging immediately rather than treating as a routine data point.

Moments of inertia are measured using a torsional pendulum or spin balance facility — though in practice the two are often the same machine. Many spin balance tables measure MOI using a built-in, non-spinning torsion pendulum mode: the spindle is locked, the object is twisted to a small angle and released, and a timer captures the decaying oscillation period that converts to MOI. Watched from across the floor, that can look enough like ordinary spin balance testing that an I&T lead may have already seen a genuine pendulum measurement without it being called out separately. A standalone, dedicated pendulum campaign is a more specialized undertaking, typically reserved for large, lightweight structures with significant deployed surface area. Spin balance itself is faster and fits naturally with spin-stabilized spacecraft — a mission architecture less common today than it once was, but still used during specific phases such as an orbit-transfer maneuver, or for science missions where continuous rotation is a useful feature. Because these facilities are specialized and not always available in-house, shipping the spacecraft off-site carries its own schedule and handling planning, including hazardous-operations considerations if propellant is already loaded — a decision worth making early in test planning, not discovering as a surprise when the measurement is due.

◆ Case Study — Magnetospheric Multiscale (MMS), 2013–2015

NASA's Magnetospheric Multiscale mission is four identically instrumented, spin-stabilized observatories with a spin-axis alignment tolerance of 0.65° — met only because each of the four flight units was individually spin-tested and trimmed at Goddard Space Flight Center before launch, rather than assumed from a shared design. The full MMS case study, including footage of the actual spin-balance test campaign, is included in the I&T Guru course material.

When a measured moment of inertia doesn't match what the attitude control team designed against, that discrepancy needs to reach the ACS team immediately, not simply get filed with the rest of the mass properties data. ACS control-law tuning and stability analysis take real time to work through a revised inertia value, and a discrepancy that surfaces late in the schedule — after the ACS team believed their analysis was closed — can become a schedule-critical item in its own right, in the same way a late-discovered mass margin problem can be.

◆ Case Study — Hitomi (ASTRO-H), 2016

JAXA's Hitomi X-ray observatory launched in February 2016 carrying a 6-meter extendable optical bench — a deployable structure that substantially changed the spacecraft's moments of inertia once extended on orbit. Updated attitude control parameters that did not correctly reflect the deployed configuration set off a runaway spin that exceeded the structure's design limits, and Hitomi was lost less than six weeks after launch. The full Hitomi case study, tracing JAXA's own failure investigation event by event, is included in the I&T Guru course material.

6. The Launch Load Environment

The spacecraft's structure must survive the mechanical loads imposed during launch — and those loads are among the most demanding the spacecraft will ever experience, often exceeding anything it will encounter again during the mission. Launch loads aren't a single environment to design against; they're four physically distinct phenomena, occurring at different points in the ascent and with very different frequency content, and that distinction matters well beyond structural theory. Each load type is typically closed through a different combination of analysis and physical test, carries a different facility footprint and schedule, and comes with a different level of risk to flight hardware if something goes wrong. Knowing which category a given load falls into is part of how an I&T lead anticipates what kind of verification activity is coming, what it will require to support, and how much margin for error the schedule actually has around it.

The four categories below also aren't independent of each other in practice. A spacecraft's own structure responds differently to a slowly varying load than it does to a load oscillating near one of its natural frequencies — which is exactly why the finite element model, modal survey testing, and coupled loads analysis discussed later in this article exist: to predict and confirm how the real structure actually responds across this whole range of load behavior, rather than treating each load type as a separate, unrelated design case.

📈
Quasi-Static Loads

Sustained accelerations during ascent, typically 3–6g axial and 0.5–2g lateral, driving sizing of primary structure in simple bending and compression. Usually closed by analysis through the coupled loads analysis process (§10) rather than a dedicated physical test — for the I&T lead, this typically shows up as an analysis product to track and accept, not a facility event to schedule.

🌊
Random Vibration

Broadband, stochastic vibration from acoustic excitation of the fairing and engine combustion noise, particularly damaging to electronic assemblies and components with resonant frequencies in the excitation band. For the I&T lead, this is a dedicated facility event with real hardware risk — a poorly notched test can damage flight hardware. See Article 09 for test methodology.

🔔
Sine / Resonant Loads

Periodic forcing at or near structural resonant frequencies, capable of driving the largest sustained structural response of any launch load. Often run on the same shaker used for random vibration; deciding which loads are closed by test versus analysis is a schedule and program-risk decision as much as a technical one.

💥
Pyrotechnic Shock

High-frequency, high-amplitude transients from separation events, propagating as stress waves to tens of kilohertz. Rarely repeatable on flight hardware — the I&T lead needs hazardous-operations review and predict-to-test criteria locked down well before the event, since there may be only one real opportunity. See Article 09 for the shock response spectrum methodology.

These load figures are not universal constants — they come from the payload user's guide of whichever launch vehicle is targeted, and smaller or newer vehicles, or different payload mass classes, often specify meaningfully different numbers. Because the specific vehicle is frequently not yet under contract when structural design begins in earnest, early design commonly proceeds against a worst-case envelope across candidate vehicles rather than one vehicle's exact figures, to preserve compatibility until a specific vehicle is secured. Early in a program, these loads exist purely as design inputs, used with the finite element model discussed next to size the spacecraft analytically before hardware exists; the testing that follows later serves two purposes at once — verifying the spacecraft survives the loads it was designed against, and separately verifying compliance with the launch vehicle's own published specifications, a contractual obligation independent of whether the spacecraft survives. Test results can also feed back into the analysis, which is part of why the CLA cycle described in §10 is iterative rather than a one-time calculation.

Representative large-vehicle quasi-static load factors are drawn from SpaceX's Falcon Payload User's Guide. Actual values vary significantly by launch vehicle, by vehicle class, and by payload mass — smaller and newer vehicles, including Rocket Lab's Electron and Neutron, publish their own distinct load specifications — and must always be confirmed against the specific vehicle's published payload user's guide for any real program.

7. The Finite Element Model — Its Role and Its Limits

The Finite Element Model (FEM) is the mathematical model of the spacecraft's structural behavior — a mesh of finite elements with defined stiffness, mass, and damping properties, approximating the structure's dynamic and static behavior. It's the primary tool for predicting natural frequencies, mode shapes, and stress under the launch load environment, and it's the foundation the structural margin calculations (§11) and coupled loads analysis (§10) both depend on directly.

A spacecraft FEM for a large mission can be a genuinely large computing problem, and every CLA cycle described in §10 requires it to be solved again — so a bigger model takes longer to turn around every time the analysis is rerun. The model isn't built once and left alone; several revision triggers come directly out of I&T's own work rather than the original design process: a measured mass, CoM, or MOI that diverges from what the FEM was built against; a late ballast addition, of the kind discussed in §3 and §4, since it changes the mass distribution the model assumes; or an anomaly during environmental testing significant enough to call the model's assumptions into question.

The FEM's predictions are only as good as the model's fidelity to the actual structure — every simplification, from a joint treated as rigid to a material property taken from a handbook rather than measured, introduces uncertainty that doesn't go away until it's checked against real hardware, which is exactly what modal survey testing (§8) exists to do.

For the I&T lead, the practical value isn't tracking correlation status or deciding when the model needs revision — that determination belongs to the mechanical lead, who owns the model. What matters is recognizing the events above that can trigger a revision, particularly the ones originating from I&T's own work, and raising it early: a revision takes real time and can mean rerunning the coupled loads analysis in §10, a schedule impact worth seeing coming rather than discovering after the fact.

⚙ The Model Is Not the Spacecraft

The FEM predicts what the structure should do if the model's assumptions are correct; the spacecraft does what it does. The two may agree closely or differ, and the degree of agreement is determined by the model's quality, not wishful thinking. Margin-positive analysis is a prediction, not a guarantee — modal survey testing is the mechanism that validates or corrects the assumptions behind it. Programs that treat FEM predictions as verified results without that confirmation are accepting unknown structural risk at launch.

8. Modal Survey Testing — Correlating the FEM

A modal survey test — also called a modal test or structural dynamics test — measures the spacecraft's actual natural frequencies, mode shapes, and damping ratios by exciting the structure and measuring the vibration response at many locations simultaneously. This is frequently performed on the Structural/Thermal Model described earlier in this series, since a non-functional test article can be exposed to qualification-level dynamic environments without risking flight electronics. The mechanical lead compares the measured parameters to FEM predictions and updates the model — material properties, joint stiffnesses, boundary conditions — until it reproduces the measured behavior within defined correlation criteria.

For the I&T lead, this is worth planning as a real facility event, not a quick check — instrumenting sometimes hundreds of sensors is often a bigger driver of schedule footprint than the data collection itself. The correlation criteria (typically 5% frequency agreement for dominant modes and a Modal Assurance Criterion above 0.9 for paired modes) are the mechanical lead's call to evaluate, not the I&T lead's. What the I&T lead needs to track is the schedule consequence of the outcome: a model that doesn't correlate cleanly may mean additional excitation points or a longer analysis cycle before the FEM is ready to support the coupled loads analysis in §10 — the same kind of downstream risk as an FEM revision trigger in §7.

9. Test Support Logistics: GSE, Handling, and Functional Verification

Every physical test described in this article — random vibration, sine or resonant testing, modal survey, and the environmental testing covered elsewhere in this series — requires more than scheduling facility time and waiting for a result. Before a spacecraft ever reaches the shaker table or the modal survey rig, its test and operating configuration must be changed, the receiving area has to be prepared, and the hardware has to be moved. All of this is squarely I&T's responsibility.

Spacecraft electronics are normally connected to EGSE for power, commanding, and telemetry, which has to be disconnected whenever the spacecraft moves to a different room, building, or facility. Whether EGSE also moves and gets reconnected before, between, or after subsequent tests depends on program philosophy, and many programs require a functional or "aliveness" check to confirm the spacecraft came through a test unchanged. The spacecraft also typically needs to be lifted onto a transportation device and then a handling or support fixture appropriate to the test to follow — often a purpose-built MGSE providing attachment points for the shaker or excitation system, distinct from whatever fixture the spacecraft has been living on day to day.

The move itself carries its own requirements. Contamination control and ESD protection don't pause because a test is coming up — if anything, a move between a cleanroom and a test cell is exactly when a lapse is most likely, because attention is on the upcoming test rather than the move.

I&T also owns the paperwork a test depends on: generating its own work orders, procedures, and test plans, and separately verifying that documentation owned by other groups — configuration drawings, software versions, calibration and certification records — is current before the test proceeds. On a well-staffed program each item has a responsible owner and the I&T lead's job is confirming readiness with them; on a smaller program, the I&T lead may need to verify some of it directly. Either way, an expired calibration certificate discovered on test day is a preventable schedule loss.

All of this consumes real schedule time that's easy to underestimate if you're only looking at the mechanical team's stated test duration. EGSE disconnect, the MGSE swap, the move itself, paperwork verification, functional checks before and after, and the move back — strung together, a "two-day test" can easily become a week-long activity on the I&T schedule. None of that reflects badly on the mechanical team's estimate; it simply isn't the whole activity, and it has to be planned into the test flow from the start — ideally during earlier program phases — rather than discovered once the test is already booked.

  • I&T owns: EGSE disconnect and reconnect, the MGSE swap, the physical move itself and its contamination and ESD protection, and any required functional or aliveness checks.
  • I&T owns: generating its own test paperwork, and verifying that documentation owned by other groups — configuration drawings, software versions, calibration and certification records — is current before the test.
  • The test facility owns: its own test equipment — the shaker or excitation system, data acquisition, and the instrumentation used to run and record the test itself.

None of this is unique to the tests described in this article — the same GSE reconfiguration, handling, and functional verification pattern applies to thermal-vacuum and EMC testing covered later in this series. It's worth building the habit here.

10. Coupled Loads Analysis

Coupled Loads Analysis (CLA) is the formal process by which the spacecraft's structural response to the launch load environment is predicted using the combined model of the spacecraft and the launch vehicle — an analysis that treats the spacecraft in isolation is not a coupled loads analysis and will not accurately predict the loads the spacecraft actually experiences.

CLA is performed by the launch vehicle provider, using its own structural dynamic model together with a reduced-order version of the spacecraft's FEM — the CLA model — that the mechanical lead prepares and delivers. The I&T lead's role is coordinating the handoff: confirming the CLA model is delivered on the date the launch vehicle provider needs it, and tracking turnaround time for results, since a late delivery on either side pushes the whole cycle. This is one of the most schedule-sensitive interfaces in any program, since it depends on two separate organizations each holding to their own commitments.

◆ The CLA Cycle and Its Schedule Implications

CLA is not a one-time activity. It is a cycle: the spacecraft program provides the CLA model; the launch vehicle provider runs the analysis; the resulting loads are compared to the structural design margins; if they exceed the margins, either the structure is redesigned or the analysis assumptions are revisited — a call made by the mechanical lead and program management, not by I&T — and the cycle repeats. The number of cycles required determines how much schedule CLA consumes. The final cycle, providing launch commit inputs, uses the correlated FEM from the modal survey test, and the schedule between correlation and final CLA closure can be a critical path item.

When a CLA cycle comes back with an exceeded margin, the I&T lead isn't deciding the fix — but the schedule consequence is worth watching closely. A structural redesign can reach back into work I&T thought was already finished, requiring rework or retest of hardware already partway through integration. Tracking the cycle count and asking early what a given outcome means for the I&T schedule is the practical hook here.

CLA is only one side of the spacecraft-to-launch-vehicle relationship; the full interface — ICDs, the payload user's guide, separation systems, and encapsulation — is covered in a dedicated article later in this series.

11. Structural Margin and the Factor of Safety

Structural margin of safety is the quantitative measure of the structure's capability relative to the loads it must sustain: (Allowable Load / Design Load) − 1, where positive margin indicates capability exceeds demand. The design load incorporates a factor of safety — a multiplier accounting for uncertainties in load prediction, material properties, and manufacturing variability.

The factor of safety a program adopts — typically 1.25 to 2.0 — is a decision made by the mechanical lead and program management, not by I&T, but it determines what kind of verification the structure needs. A structure tested to the qualification load without failure has demonstrated positive margin through hardware — a real facility event I&T has to plan and support. A structure with positive analytical margin but no test verification has only analysis confidence; knowing which path a given structure is on early is what lets the I&T lead plan the right facility time, or correctly plan for none.

A negative margin discovered at any point enters the program's formal anomaly management process, covered elsewhere in this series. For I&T, the consequence mirrors §10's exceeded-margin case: a negative margin can mean redesign, rework, or requalification of hardware already partway through integration, and the schedule impact is what's worth tracking closely.

12. Mass Properties at Launch — The Final Accounting

The launch authority requires, as one of its Launch Readiness Review inputs, a complete mass properties statement for the spacecraft in its launch configuration — wet mass, CoM location in all three axes, and moments of inertia about all three principal axes — used to confirm the spacecraft is within the launch vehicle's payload envelope and to set its flight control system parameters. The statement is compiled from the measured dry mass (§3), the measured propellant mass, the calculated propellant CoM contribution, and the measured dry CoM and moments of inertia (§4, §5). Assembling it is the mass properties engineer's responsibility, not I&T's — but every input feeding it is a measurement or test result I&T had a direct hand in producing, which is why this final section closes the loop on everything the article has covered.

What makes this deliverable different is its schedule position. An FEM revision or a CLA cycle can, within limits, absorb some schedule slip; the LRR mass properties statement can't — it's due by a specific date tied to the launch campaign, and how unforgiving that date is depends on whether the mission has a flexible window (weeks of acceptable dates) or a fixed, narrow one (driven by orbital mechanics, where missing it can mean years' delay). Either way, the statement depends on every measured input in this article already being complete and correct by the time it's assembled. For the I&T lead, the takeaway is to work backward from the actual launch window early in test planning and confirm every measurement this statement depends on is scheduled to close with real margin ahead of it.

The uncertainty of each input is propagated through the calculation, and the program must demonstrate that even the worst-case combination leaves the spacecraft within the payload envelope and the ACS operating assumptions. If it doesn't, that's a late-discovered problem with nowhere left to hide it — unlike a margin issue caught mid-program (§10, §11), a violation caught at this final stage has essentially no schedule left to absorb rework.

Summary

Mass properties — mass, center of mass, and moments of inertia — are not bookkeeping. They are engineering inputs that determine whether a launch vehicle can deliver a spacecraft to orbit, whether its attitude control system can point and maneuver correctly, and whether its structure survives the loads of launch. Mass is tracked continuously and in detail throughout a program; CoM and MOI are measured far less often and rely on characterized fixtures and disciplined configuration management to remain trustworthy between measurements. On the structural side, the finite element model, modal survey testing, and coupled loads analysis form an iterative cycle: predict, measure, correlate, and — where necessary — revise, closing only when the structure's margin against the launch load environment is demonstrated with confidence rather than assumed.

None of this is theoretical. Hitomi shows what happens when an attitude control system operates on mass properties data that no longer matches the spacecraft's real configuration. MMS shows the opposite: four spacecraft that met a demanding spin-balance tolerance because the measurement and trim discipline was built into the plan from day one, not left to chance. JWST shows that even a flagship program with deep technical resources can be forced into an expensive, schedule-consuming recovery once mass growth margin runs out. For the I&T lead, the throughline across all of it is the same: know what's being measured, know why it matters, and make sure the schedule and resources exist to do it right the first time.

Key Terms Used in This Article

TermDefinition
Beginning-of-Life (BOL) MassThe spacecraft's mass after some propellant is expended but before mission operations begin. Determines the attitude control authority available at the start of the mission operations phase.
Center of Mass (CoM)The point about which the spacecraft's mass is evenly distributed — the reference point for attitude dynamics. A CoM not at its expected location degrades attitude control performance.
CLA ModelA reduced-order version of the spacecraft's finite element model, provided by the spacecraft program to the launch vehicle provider as the input to coupled loads analysis.
Coupled Loads Analysis (CLA)The prediction of spacecraft structural loads using a combined model of the spacecraft and launch vehicle, accounting for the dynamic coupling between them. Required input to the launch vehicle compatibility verification.
End-of-Life (EOL) MassThe spacecraft's mass once its operational mission is complete — propellant may be fully depleted, or a reserve may remain, specifically set aside for a planned deorbit or disposal maneuver. Determines the final orbital parameters of the spacecraft and whether any deorbit or disposal requirement can be met.
Factor of SafetyA multiplier applied to predicted limit loads to account for uncertainties in loads, material properties, and manufacturing variability. Typically 1.25–2.0 for spacecraft primary structure.
Finite Element Model (FEM)A mathematical model of the spacecraft's structural behavior, discretized into finite elements with defined stiffness and mass properties. The primary tool for predicting natural frequencies, mode shapes, and stress under launch loads.
Launch MassThe spacecraft's total mass at liftoff — bus, instruments, propellant, pressurant, and ordnance. Together with margin, determines which launch vehicles are viable candidates for the mission, and later, whether the selected vehicle can deliver the spacecraft to its target orbit as required.
MAC (Modal Assurance Criterion)A scalar measure (0 to 1) of the degree of correlation between two mode shapes. Values above 0.9 for paired modes are typically required for FEM correlation acceptance.
Margin of SafetyA quantitative measure of structural capability relative to demand: (Allowable Load / Design Load) − 1. Positive margin indicates capability exceeds demand; negative margin indicates predicted failure.
Modal Survey TestA test that measures the spacecraft's actual natural frequencies, mode shapes, and damping ratios by exciting the structure and measuring the response. Used to validate and update the FEM.
Moments of InertiaThe rotational equivalents of mass — the resistance of the spacecraft to angular acceleration about each of its three principal axes. Critical inputs to attitude control system sizing and performance analysis.
Spin BalanceA facility that measures a spacecraft's moments of inertia by spinning it about a known axis and measuring the resulting dynamic response. An alternative to torsional pendulum measurement.
Torsional PendulumA facility that measures a spacecraft's moments of inertia by suspending it from a torsion rod and measuring the oscillation period as it swings.
Weighing FixtureA purpose-built support structure with calibrated load cells at defined support points, used to measure spacecraft mass and center of mass without placing the spacecraft directly on a scale.
