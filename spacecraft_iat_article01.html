Every spacecraft I&T campaign begins the same way: with a blank sheet of paper and a question. Given what this spacecraft has to do, given the environment it has to survive, given the schedule and budget available — what exactly do we need to build, integrate, and test, in what order, and how do we know when we're done?

The document that answers that question is the I&T Plan. It is the constitution of the entire test program. It establishes the philosophy, defines the scope, describes the approach, and allocates responsibility. A good I&T Plan anticipates the hard decisions before they become emergencies. A poor one means that every hard decision gets made under schedule pressure, without the benefit of prior deliberation.

"The I&T Plan is not a schedule. It is not a procedure. It is the engineering argument for why your test program is sufficient to justify flight. It should be written — and challenged — with that standard in mind."

1. What the I&T Plan Must Accomplish

An I&T Plan is not a schedule, though it informs one. It is not a test procedure, though it defines the framework from which procedures are derived. It is an engineering argument — a structured case that the test program, taken as a whole, is sufficient to verify that the spacecraft meets its requirements and is ready to fly. That argument must address four fundamental questions:

  • What are we trying to prove? The test program must be traceable to mission and system requirements. Every test must exist because something needs to be verified. Tests performed "because we always do this" without a clear requirement link cannot be defended when schedule pressure demands cuts — and schedule pressure always comes.
  • How will we prove it? This covers the approach: which tests at which integration levels, which test environments, what instrumentation, and what constitutes success. For verification tests, success means demonstrating compliance against defined pass/fail criteria. For characterization tests — thermal model correlation, instrument calibration, performance baseline measurement — success means acquiring data of defined quality. Both are legitimate I&T activities; both must be planned with equal rigor.
  • What flight configuration will be tested? The principle of test-as-you-fly requires that the configuration under test be as close as possible to the flight configuration. The I&T Plan must identify and justify any deviations.
  • How will we know we are done? The Plan defines the completion criteria: what data must be collected, what anomalies must be resolved, what reviews must be held before the spacecraft can be declared ready for launch.

2. The Levels of Integration — From Parts to Observatory

The most fundamental organizational concept in any I&T program is the integration level hierarchy. Spacecraft hardware is assembled in stages, and each stage has its own integration and test requirements. The hierarchy determines the sequence of the entire program, the allocation of test resources, and the strategy for finding problems early, when they are cheapest and easiest to fix.

⚙ Why Levels Matter

Integration creates emergent behaviors. Two circuit boards that each pass their individual acceptance tests can still fail when connected, due to impedance mismatches, ground loops, or software timing issues that only appear in the combined system. Two subsystems that each pass thermal-vacuum testing in isolation can interact thermally in unexpected ways when mounted together on the spacecraft bus. Testing at each level is the only way to find these interactions before they become on-orbit anomalies.

L0⚛️
Piece Parts & Materials
FoundationParts Engineering
Individual materials and piece parts: electronic components, fasteners, adhesives, coatings, lubricants, and raw structural materials. Flight programs subject piece parts to screening to identify early failures before they are incorporated into assemblies. Problems found here — a bad component lot, a counterfeit material, an incompatible lubricant — are infinitely cheaper to address at this level than after incorporation into a flight assembly.
Typical I&T Activities Incoming inspection · Lot acceptance testing · Radiation testing · Destructive physical analysis (DPA) · Material certifications
L1🔧
Assemblies & Circuit Card Assemblies
ManufacturingWorkmanship
Piece parts assembled into functional units: populated circuit card assemblies, bonded structural panels, small mechanisms, harness segments. I&T activities focus on workmanship verification and basic functionality. Spacecraft electronics are typically required to meet IPC-A-610 Class 3 workmanship standards, intended for high-reliability electronics where continued performance is critical.
Typical I&T Activities X-ray inspection · Electrical continuity · Dimensional inspection · Workmanship audit
L2📦
Components & Electronics Units
Component I&TEnvironmental TestQual vs. Acceptance
Assemblies integrated into complete electronic units, mechanisms, and structural assemblies: a flight computer, a power conditioning unit, a reaction wheel, a star tracker. This is the first level at which comprehensive environmental testing is typically performed. Component-level testing screens out latent workmanship defects and verifies the unit can survive launch and the space environment. This is where the critical distinction between qualification testing (on a qual unit, at elevated levels) and acceptance testing (on every flight unit, at flight levels) is most sharply drawn.
Typical I&T Activities Comprehensive functional testing · Vibration testing · Thermal cycling & thermal-vacuum · EMC testing · Mass properties verification
L3🔌
Subsystems
Interface VerificationFunctional
Components integrated into functional subsystems: the Electrical Power Subsystem, the Attitude Determination and Control Subsystem, Command and Data Handling, Telecommunications, Propulsion, Thermal Control. Subsystem-level I&T is where interface verification between components becomes the primary focus. Programs that skip formal subsystem testing find these interface problems at the spacecraft level, under more pressure, in a more complex configuration.
Typical I&T Activities Interface verification · Closed-loop functional testing · Subsystem thermal-vacuum · Software integration testing
L4🔭
Instruments & Payload
Science PayloadCalibrationCompatibility
Science instruments and hosted payloads, typically developed by a separate team and delivered as completed, tested units. The interface between instrument and spacecraft bus is one of the highest-risk interfaces in the program. Mechanical, electrical, thermal, and data interfaces must all be verified — ideally using a fit check and a compatibility test well before the instrument is delivered for spacecraft integration.
Typical I&T Activities Instrument-level functional testing · Calibration campaigns · Fit checks · Electrical compatibility testing · Contamination monitoring
⚠ The Instrument Delivery Problem

Late instrument or payload delivery is one of the most common causes of spacecraft I&T schedule compression. The pattern is familiar: the instrument slips, the spacecraft integration schedule is held, and then both are compressed together to hit the launch date — placing the program under maximum pressure at exactly the moment requiring maximum care. The correct response is to identify early which I&T activities are schedule-critical, which have float, and which can be deferred to the launch site. This planning must be done before the payload slips — not after.

L5🛸
Spacecraft
System-Level I&TFull Environmental Suite
Subsystems, instruments, and payload elements integrated into the complete spacecraft. Spacecraft-level testing is where the payoff of the entire lower-level test program is realized — or where its gaps become apparent. Problems that escaped detection at component and subsystem level are found here, in the most time-consuming test environment of the campaign. Every problem found at the subsystem level is one that does not have to be found on the spacecraft.
Typical I&T Activities System functional testing · Mass properties measurement · System-level EMC · Vibration & acoustic testing · Thermal-vacuum · End-to-end RF · Propulsion leak testing
L6🌌
Observatory
Flagship ClassDeployablesCryo-Optical
For the most complex space science missions — large observatories, flagship planetary spacecraft, multi-element systems — there is a level above the spacecraft. An observatory typically consists of a spacecraft bus, multiple science instruments, perhaps a sunshield or mission-enabling structure, and deployable elements that cannot be fully tested in their flight configuration on the ground. The James Webb Space Telescope is the canonical example: JWST comprised an optical telescope assembly, an integrated science instrument module containing four science instruments, a spacecraft bus, and a sunshield roughly the size of a tennis court — all folded for launch and deployed in sequence at L2. No facility on Earth was large enough to test the fully deployed observatory in a flight-like thermal-vacuum environment. JWST launched in December 2021 and has exceeded its science performance requirements on orbit.
Typical I&T Activities Deployment testing with gravity offload · Cryo-optical testing · Observatory-level thermal-vacuum · Multi-element interface verification · Configuration management at maximum complexity
✦ Observatory-Level Challenges — The JWST Example

Deployable structures: The sunshield and mirror deployment sequences could be tested on the ground, but only with gravity-offload systems that introduce their own uncertainties. The test program had to characterize and account for those uncertainties explicitly. Cryogenic testing: JWST's instruments operate at temperatures as low as 7 Kelvin. The only facility large enough to thermal-vacuum test the telescope and instruments was Chamber A at NASA Johnson Space Center — requiring significant modification from its Apollo-era configuration. Configuration control: With a program spanning more than two decades of development, maintaining precise knowledge of the as-tested vs. as-flown configuration across thousands of components required extraordinary configuration management discipline — a lesson applicable to any long-duration program.

3. The Model Philosophy — STM, EM, QM, PFM, FM

One of the earliest and most consequential decisions in any spacecraft program is the model philosophy — how many physical versions of the spacecraft will be built, and what role each plays in the verification program. The model philosophy determines hardware procurement, test campaign structure, design risk retirement strategy, and — critically — how much margin the program has when anomalies occur.

The multiple-model approach was not invented as best practice. It was an engineering necessity driven by the conditions of the early space program, where every subsystem was a first-generation design with unknown failure modes and immature manufacturing processes. When you do not know how something will fail, you build several and test them to find out. As flight heritage accumulated, the justification for multiple models weakened for systems with established heritage — and the industry shifted toward protoflight testing for programs where the design, environment, and manufacturing processes were well characterized. The lesson of the 1990s "faster, better, cheaper" era is that this shift must be matched to the actual state of knowledge about the design, not to the budget available for hardware. The model philosophy is a risk management decision, not a procurement decision.

STM
Structural / Thermal Model
Verify structural integrity and thermal design under environmental loads before flight hardware exists
Build Standard

Structurally and thermally representative; non-functional electrically. May use lower-grade materials where structural properties match flight.

Test Exposure

Full qualification-level vibration, acoustic, and thermal balance testing. May be tested to failure or beyond qualification to explore design margins.

Fate After Test

Retained for reference; sometimes refurbished as a ground test bed. Not flown.

EM
Engineering Model
Verify electrical design, functional performance, software interfaces, and integration procedures before flight hardware is committed
Build Standard

Electrically representative using flight-equivalent components. Mechanical fidelity varies — may be form-factor representative or engineering-grade structure.

Test Exposure

Full functional testing; software integration and validation; EGSE compatibility; interface testing with other subsystems. May undergo limited environmental test at acceptance levels.

Fate After Test

Retained as a ground testbed (FlatSat) for flight software development, anomaly investigation, and operations support throughout the mission.

QM
Qualification Model
Demonstrate design margins by undergoing environmental test at levels more severe than the flight unit will experience
Build Standard

Built to full flight specification — identical to the flight model in design, materials, and workmanship.

Test Exposure

Full qualification-level environmental test: vibration, acoustics, TVAC, EMC — all at qualification levels (1.25×–1.5× flight) and qualification durations (2×–4× flight durations).

Fate After Test

Not flown. The qualification test consumes a significant fraction of the hardware's fatigue life. Retained for reference.

FM
Flight Model
The unit that flies — undergoes acceptance-level environmental test only, verifying workmanship without consuming design margin
Build Standard

Full flight specification. Flight-qualified components from controlled lots. Full flight-quality workmanship with QA inspection at every stage.

Test Exposure

Acceptance-level environmental test only: vibration, acoustics, T-V, EMC — all at flight levels and acceptance durations. Screens for workmanship defects; does not demonstrate design margins.

Fate After Test

Launched.

PFM
Protoflight Model
A single unit serving as both qualification and flight model — tested at qualification levels but acceptance durations, then flown
Build Standard

Full flight specification — identical to a flight model in every respect.

Test Exposure

Protoflight-level environmental test: qualification-level amplitudes with acceptance-level durations. A deliberate compromise between design-margin verification and preserving the hardware for flight.

Fate After Test

Launched. The protoflight unit is the flight unit — there is no backup.

The three principal approaches are the full qualification approach (STM + EM + QM + FM) — standard for NASA flagship and ESA cornerstone missions where failure is unacceptable; the protoflight approach (STM + EM + PFM) — standard for Class B/C missions and commercial spacecraft where cost constraints preclude a dedicated qualification unit; and the minimal approach (EM + PFM or PFM only) — standard for programs with strong heritage and aggressive cost targets. The EM, regardless of which approach is chosen, is the program's most important risk-reduction investment and should never be eliminated to save cost.

⚠ The Protoflight Trap

The protoflight approach is attractive because it saves the cost and schedule of a dedicated qualification unit. It is also the approach that produces the most severe consequences when things go wrong — because the unit being tested is the unit that must fly. If the protoflight test reveals a design deficiency, the program must repair the flight unit, re-test it, and accept the residual fatigue life consumed by the original test and the repair. There is no backup qualification unit that absorbed the damage instead. Programs that choose the protoflight approach must do so with clear understanding that they are accepting the risk that a problem which would have been discovered on a QM and corrected before the FM was built will instead be discovered on the only flight article.

4. The Phases of an I&T Campaign

An I&T campaign does not begin when the first hardware arrives on the test floor. It begins during the design phase, when the I&T approach is first defined, and it ends formally at the launch readiness review. A common and costly misreading of the phase structure is to treat I&T as a Phase D activity — to staff the function late, defer the planning, and assume that experienced test personnel can be recruited and made effective on short notice. The decisions that determine the quality of a Phase D campaign are made in Phases A, B, and C.

A program that defers I&T investment does not save time or money — it borrows against Phase D at a very high rate of interest. By the time hardware arrives on the test floor, it is too late to correct the consequences of decisions that should have been made at the design table.

Phase Name Key I&T Activities Gate / Exit Criteria If Deferred to Phase D
Phase A Concept Study I&T concept defined; test philosophy established; top-level requirements derived; preliminary assessment of test facility needs Preliminary I&T Plan approved; philosophy reviewed at SRR Requirements written without regard to how they will be verified; unverifiable requirements discovered too late to change
Phase B Preliminary Design I&T Plan baselined; integration levels and test flow defined; EGSE concept developed; test facility selection initiated; long-lead test equipment identified I&T Plan baselined at PDR; EGSE procurement authorized EGSE not ready when hardware arrives; test facility contracts placed too late; integration sequence undefined until hardware is on the floor
Phase C Final Design Detailed test procedures developed; EGSE fabricated and tested; test facility contracts awarded; component and subsystem test campaigns begin Test procedures reviewed at CDR; EGSE qualification complete Test procedures written under schedule pressure with no margin for review; subsystem problems not found until system-level integration
Phase D Integration & Test All integration activities; component, subsystem, and system-level environmental testing; anomaly resolution; launch campaign; launch site I&T System-level test complete; all anomalies dispositioned; LRR passed The bill comes due. Every deferred decision, every absent expert, every untested interface becomes an anomaly, a schedule slip, or a launch risk
Phase E Operations Launch and early operations; initial on-orbit checkout; instrument commissioning; performance verification against pre-launch baseline Mission operations readiness review; science commissioning complete On-orbit anomalies with no ground test baseline; commissioning surprises that thorough I&T would have found

5. The Governing Documents

A spacecraft I&T campaign generates and consumes a substantial body of documentation. With experience it becomes clear that the documentation architecture is the engineering memory of the program — every test performed, every anomaly found, every configuration change made, captured in a way that supports traceability, retrievability, and institutional memory.

DocumentPurpose
I&T PlanTop-level document defining the I&T philosophy, scope, approach, and organization. The "constitution" of the test program.
Test Requirements Document (TRD)Traces each test requirement to its source mission or system requirement. Provides the formal basis for the test program.
Interface Control Document (ICD)Defines the mechanical, electrical, thermal, and data interfaces between subsystems and between the spacecraft and ground systems.
Test ProceduresStep-by-step instructions for performing each test. Must be reviewed and approved before execution; cannot be deviated from without a formal change process.
Test ReportsFormal record of each test campaign: objectives, configuration, results, anomalies, and disposition. The permanent record that a test occurred and what it showed.
Nonconformance Reports (NCRs)Formal documentation of any hardware or software condition that does not meet its requirements. Every NCR must be formally closed before flight.
Discrepancy Reports (DRs)Documentation of anomalies observed during test operations. The formal problem-tracking system.
Configuration Status Accounting (CSA)The formal record of the as-built and as-tested configuration of every flight hardware item.
Launch Readiness Matrix (LRM)The pre-launch checklist: every required test, every open NCR/DR, every open action item — tracked to closure as a condition of launch authorization.

"An anomaly that is not written down does not exist in the program record. An anomaly that is written down but not formally closed is an open risk at launch. The documentation discipline of a test program is a direct reflection of its engineering culture."

6. The Technical Reviews

At each major program transition, a formal technical review is conducted. These reviews are not rubber stamps — they are structured examinations of readiness to proceed, conducted by a board that includes personnel independent of the performing team. They force the program to demonstrate readiness against defined criteria and provide an external check on the program's self-assessment.

  • System Requirements Review (SRR) — Confirms mission requirements are understood and the top-level I&T approach is consistent with them. Phase A.
  • Preliminary Design Review (PDR) — Reviews the preliminary I&T Plan and test requirements baseline. Authorizes development of detailed test procedures and EGSE. Phase B.
  • Critical Design Review (CDR) — Reviews the detailed I&T Plan, test procedures, and EGSE design. Authorizes fabrication of flight hardware and procurement of test facilities. Phase C.
  • Test Readiness Review (TRR) — Conducted before each major test campaign to confirm the test configuration is correct, procedures are approved, the facility is certified, and the team is prepared. A TRR is a launch condition — you cannot start a major test without one.
  • Pre-Ship Review (PSR) — Conducted before the spacecraft is shipped to the launch site. Confirms all home-facility I&T activities are complete and the spacecraft is in its approved configuration.
  • Launch Readiness Review (LRR) — The final gate before launch. Reviews the complete I&T campaign, confirms all open items are closed or dispositioned, and formally authorizes the launch.
⚙ On the Value of Independent Reviews

Every program believes it knows its own hardware better than any outside reviewer. This is correct, and it misses the point. The value of an independent review board is not that reviewers know the hardware better than the team. It is that they have not been living with the program long enough to have normalized its anomalies, rationalized its risks, or stopped questioning its assumptions. Fresh eyes catch things that familiarity obscures. The programs that treat their review boards as adversaries to be managed are the ones that most need the reviews to work.

7. Ground Support Equipment — The Invisible Infrastructure

No discussion of I&T program architecture is complete without addressing Ground Support Equipment (GSE) — the infrastructure that makes spacecraft integration and test possible. GSE divides into four categories: Mechanical GSE (MGSE) — lifting fixtures, transportation containers, handling dollies, test stands, and specialized tooling; Electrical GSE (EGSE) — the electrical systems that power, command, and monitor the spacecraft during ground testing; Software GSE (SWGSE) — the software systems that control test campaigns, process telemetry, and manage test data; and Special Purpose GSE — mission-specific equipment such as sensor stimulators, star field simulators, or grappling fixtures.

EGSE deserves particular emphasis. The fidelity of EGSE directly determines the fidelity of the test program. An EGSE that does not accurately simulate the spacecraft's flight interfaces will produce test results that do not accurately predict flight performance. This is not a subtle point — it has been the root cause of major on-orbit anomalies on multiple programs. EGSE design, development, and verification must be treated as an engineering program in its own right, with the same rigor applied to it as to the flight hardware itself. EGSE development must begin in Phase B — programs that defer EGSE development discover they are trying to test flight hardware with immature test equipment, which is exactly backwards from any rational risk posture.

8. Test Facility Qualification and Readiness

The spacecraft is not the only hardware in the test chamber that must be qualified. The test facility itself — the thermal-vacuum chamber, the vibration shaker, the acoustic chamber — is a complex engineering system whose performance directly determines the validity of test results. A vibration shaker that cannot deliver the specified input spectrum produces data that does not verify the requirement. A thermal-vacuum chamber that cannot reach the required vacuum level produces an environment not specified in the test procedure.

Vibration facility qualification verifies force capacity, frequency range and spectral accuracy, cross-axis performance, and control system calibration. Thermal-vacuum chamber qualification verifies ultimate vacuum level, leak rate, thermal shroud performance, cleanliness, and solar simulator performance where applicable. Acoustic chamber qualification verifies overall sound pressure level capacity, spectral shape accuracy, spatial uniformity, and reverberation time. Before every test campaign involving flight hardware, the facility undergoes a readiness verification — a functional check confirming it can deliver the required environment — reviewed as part of the Test Readiness Review.

⚙ Chamber Bakeout — The Contamination Problem

A thermal-vacuum chamber opened to atmosphere absorbs water vapor and volatile organic compounds from the ambient environment. When evacuated, these contaminants outgas from the chamber walls and can deposit on the test article, corrupting contamination cleanliness and thermal test results. The standard mitigation is a chamber bakeout — the empty chamber is evacuated and the shrouds heated (typically +60°C to +80°C) to accelerate outgassing until the residual gas analyzer shows the outgassing rate has fallen below the specified threshold. Duration ranges from 24 hours for a small clean chamber to several weeks for a large chamber idle for months. Shortening the bakeout to save schedule is a false economy — contamination deposited on the test article may require cleaning that damages delicate surfaces, or may corrupt thermal test results that cannot be recovered.

9. Building the I&T Plan: Practical Guidance

Start With Requirements, Not Test Ideas

The most common error in I&T planning is starting with a list of tests and then trying to find requirements that justify them, rather than starting with requirements and deriving the tests that verify them. Requirements-driven test planning produces a test program with clear justification for every element, unambiguous pass/fail criteria, and complete supporting evidence. Test-idea-driven planning produces a test program that is hard to defend when schedule pressure demands cuts.

Characterization Testing — When There Are No Pass/Fail Criteria

A significant fraction of the test program — sometimes 20 to 30% of the total test hours on observatory-class missions — consists of characterization testing: measurements performed not to verify compliance with a requirement, but to understand the spacecraft's actual performance, calibrate its instruments, populate its operational models, or establish baselines for comparison throughout the mission. Thermal balance testing, instrument calibration campaigns, propulsion system characterization, and star tracker field-of-view mapping are all characterization tests. In none of these cases does the test produce a simple pass or fail. It produces data that the mission will use operationally.

✦ Characterization Is I&T — Here's Why

The absence of a binary pass/fail criterion does not mean the test lacks rigor or acceptance criteria. A characterization test that produces noisy, incomplete, or unrepeatable data has failed — not because the hardware is defective, but because the test did not achieve its purpose. The acceptance criteria for characterization testing typically include measurement uncertainty within a defined bound, data coverage across the full operating range, repeatability between successive measurement sets, and completeness of the data record needed by the downstream consumer. These criteria are defined in the test procedure and reviewed at the TRR, just as pass/fail criteria are. Characterization data enters the formal program record — the thermal model correlation report, the calibration database, the propulsion performance summary — and is the quantitative foundation on which mission operations and science performance depend.

Plan for Anomalies

Every I&T campaign will have anomalies. The I&T Plan must describe the anomaly management process: what constitutes an anomaly, how anomalies are classified, what investigation process applies to each class, and what authority is required to close each class. Programs that do not plan for anomaly management find themselves improvising the process in the middle of a test campaign, under schedule pressure.

Configuration Control Is Not Optional

The I&T Plan must define how the as-built and as-tested configuration will be controlled and documented. The ability to answer "what exactly are we testing right now?" is essential to anomaly investigation, launch readiness assessment, and on-orbit anomaly support. Programs that treat configuration control as a paperwork burden pay for that attitude when they need to answer that question under pressure.

⚡ A Word on Schedule Pressure

Every I&T program experiences schedule pressure. The I&T Plan should be written with this in mind: every element should be defensible under pressure. When a program manager asks "can we skip this test?", the answer should not be "the plan says we have to do it." The answer should be "yes, but here is what requirement goes unverified, here is the failure mode we would be accepting, and here is the margin we are consuming." That is an engineering conversation. Engineers who can have that conversation — who understand the rationale for every element of their test program — are the ones who maintain the integrity of the I&T campaign when it is under the most pressure.

Summary

The I&T Plan and the program architecture it defines are the foundation of the entire spacecraft integration and test campaign. The levels of integration — piece parts through observatory — are not administrative constructs. They reflect a physical reality: integration creates emergent behaviors that can only be found and resolved at the level where they emerge. Testing at every level is not redundancy. It is risk management. The model philosophy determines how much margin the program has when those findings occur. The phase structure determines whether the program arrives at the test floor with mature infrastructure or is still developing it. And the governing documents and reviews are the mechanisms that keep the entire structure honest.
