Of all the environments that a spacecraft must survive, the thermal environment of space is simultaneously the most pervasive and the most mission-specific. In space, there is no air to conduct heat away from the electronics, no convection to equalize temperatures across the structure, and no ground nearby to absorb radiated energy, so every watt a spacecraft dissipates must be managed with deliberate design. Every component on the spacecraft — from the cryogenic detector operating at 40 Kelvin to the battery pack that must never exceed 30°C — has a temperature range within which it must operate. The thermal design of the spacecraft is the system that maintains every one of those components within its required range for the duration of the mission. Thermal-vacuum (T-V) testing is how we verify, before launch, that the thermal design actually works and that the spacecraft built to it performs as intended.

Thermal-vacuum (T-V) testing is technically among the most demanding tests in the entire I&T program. The test requires the largest, most complex facilities, and no other test takes as long: a system-level T-V campaign typically runs three to six weeks or longer. Round-the-clock staffing is mandatory. T-V testing also combines the challenges of vacuum operations, extreme temperature management, functional test execution, and real-time anomaly resolution in a way that taxes every element of the program's engineering and operations capability simultaneously.

The whole project team is needed, at any hour. Thermal engineering is the obvious need, but every spacecraft component and subsystem team, flight software, quality assurance, safety, facility operations, IT, systems engineering, and even program management must be on site or reachable for the duration of the test. No other activity in the program focuses a team this large, and the expense and complexity match. Time cannot be lost waiting for the right person to answer a question, investigate an anomaly, or make a decision that can affect not only the test but the viability, safety, and survival of the spacecraft under test.

When it is done well, T-V testing provides the highest-confidence verification that the spacecraft's thermal design is correct and that the spacecraft as actually built performs as the design intends. A thermal design can be adequate and the hardware still perform in unexpected ways or have previously undiscovered faults. Coatings or blankets may have been installed incorrectly, a temperature sensor may be dead or misplaced, an oscillator may drift with temperature, or a latent defect may surface in a unit that passed every earlier test. Section 2 covers these as-built objectives in detail.

The test also gives the flight operations team their first look at the spacecraft in action, and a preview of what they are likely to see in orbit, at least in some operating modes. When T-V testing is truncated, poorly instrumented, or executed with an incorrect thermal model, it can provide a false assurance that sends hardware to launch with unresolved thermal risks or undiscovered defects.

⚙ A Note on Expertise: Planning and Leading the Test, Not Owning the Thermal Design.

Thermal-vacuum testing puts the I&T lead at the center of the largest team the program will assemble for a single activity. Unlike the mechanical environments covered in Article 09, the test is not a sequence of discrete runs separated by inspections. The spacecraft is powered, commanded, and monitored for weeks on end, sealed inside a facility where nobody can put a hand on the hardware, and the people who understand each part of it must be reachable at any hour. Every decision has to account for that: how the chamber is configured, how EGSE reaches the spacecraft through the chamber wall, how shifts are staffed and handed over around the clock, who is on call for each subsystem, which functional tests run at which plateau, and what happens to the schedule when an anomaly appears at 3 a.m. on a cold plateau.

Doing that job well means knowing enough about the thermal side to ask the right questions and recognize a problem early. Is the hot plateau stable, and is the facility on its LN2 timeline? Is an out-of-family temperature a test-setup artifact or a flight design issue? What does an open heater circuit on the cold soak mean for the rest of the sequence and for launch? The thermal mathematical model, test levels, plateau definitions, thermocouple map, heater setpoints, and correlation belong to the thermal discipline, contamination requirements belong to contamination engineering, and each subsystem team owns the behavior of its own hardware. The I&T lead's role is to make sure the right expert is reachable for every question and decision, that each discipline's objectives are achievable inside a test that also has to meet everyone else's, and that those objectives are protected when schedule pressure asks which ones can be cut.

None of this can be improvised once the chamber door closes. The planning starts months in advance, so that test procedures, staffing schedules, thermal-vacuum-compatible cables, instrumentation, fixtures, heaters, cold and hot targets, stimulators, and the rest are all prepared and ready on time. Not every one of those items is the I&T lead's to determine. Knowing what the test needs, who must prepare or provide each item, and assuring that everything comes together on schedule is the I&T lead's job.

✦ The Same Questions, Every Time

Every environmental test the I&T lead runs comes back to the same five questions. In thermal-vacuum testing each one carries more weight than anywhere else in the program, because the answers have to hold for weeks of continuous operation behind a sealed chamber door.

  • Why is this test being done? A T-V campaign serves several objectives at once: model correlation, margin verification, functional performance at temperature, as-built verification and workmanship screening, heater verification, outgassing and bake-out, and operations familiarization. They compete for the same chamber time, so know which ones are non-negotiable before the timeline is finalized.
  • What changes electrically? Everything the EGSE does in the cleanroom now has to reach the spacecraft through vacuum feedthroughs, alongside test heater power and control, facility data acquisition, and hundreds of thermocouple channels.
  • What changes mechanically? This covers the thermal stand or chamber fixture, heater plates and shrouds, the thermocouple installation campaign, and the lift into the chamber. Every piece of MGSE inside the door must survive vacuum and temperature and must not become a contamination source or an unmodeled thermal path.
  • What are the contamination, ESD, move, and shipping risks? If the chamber is in the same building or complex, the move is short: the spacecraft goes from the cleanroom to a previously certified clean chamber area, typically contamination-bagged and purged for the trip. If the chamber is in another building, across town, or in another state, the move becomes a transportation campaign much like launch site preparation (Article 15), usually followed by a post-arrival functional test in a second cleanroom before the spacecraft enters the chamber. Either way, the chamber facility's cleanliness must be certified beforehand, and the arrangements, preparation, and MGSE can become elaborate.
  • Can the schedule, staffing, and contingency plan hold? The campaign runs around the clock for weeks, with real people who get tired, get sick, and have lives outside the test. The chamber was booked years in advance. Calibrations, certifications, and facility maintenance must remain current through the end of the test, not just at its start. The criteria for breaking vacuum have to be agreed before pumpdown, not invented during an anomaly.

The thermal engineer owns the answers to the technical questions inside each of these. The I&T lead owns making all five work together. Section 8 covers the campaign from that side.

1. The Space Thermal Environment

Understanding why thermal-vacuum testing is necessary requires a clear picture of the space thermal environment and how it differs from anything a spacecraft experiences on the ground. In space, there are only two mechanisms of heat transfer: radiation and conduction. Convection — the dominant heat transfer mechanism in any air-filled environment — is absent. This single fact changes everything about spacecraft thermal design.

Representative Temperatures in the Space Thermal Environment
<10 K Deep-cryogenic detectors, operating against a ~3 K space background JWST MIRI (~7 K)
40 to 80 K Cryogenic science detectors; passive cooler focal planes IR detector arrays
−20 to +50°C Typical electronics operating range; spacecraft bus components Flight computers, batteries
+80 to +120°C Sun-facing structural panels; solar array cells at 1 AU MLI outer layer, S/A substrates
>200°C Thruster nozzles; hardware exposed to close solar approach Parker Solar Probe heat shield (~1,400°C sun side)

A spacecraft in Earth orbit sees three external heat inputs: direct sunlight, sunlight reflected from the Earth (albedo), and infrared energy emitted by the Earth itself. As it moves in and out of eclipse, it experiences alternating heating and cooling. In a typical low Earth orbit, eclipses last up to roughly 35 minutes of a roughly 90-minute orbit, cycling continuously. The actual eclipse duration depends on the orbit and its orientation relative to the Sun. Some orbits see little or no eclipse for long periods, while geosynchronous spacecraft see none for most of the year and then pass through eclipse seasons with daily shadow periods of over an hour. The spacecraft structure, its components, and its thermal management hardware (heaters, heat pipes, radiators, multilayer insulation) must manage these inputs to keep every component within its thermal limits throughout the mission. The specific orbit determines which hot and cold conditions the thermal-vacuum test has to reproduce.

In deep space, the thermal environment is driven primarily by solar distance. At 1 AU (Earth's distance from the Sun), the solar constant is approximately 1,361 W/m2. At Mars (1.52 AU), it is about 590 W/m2. At Jupiter (5.2 AU), it is only 51 W/m2 - roughly 4% of the level at Earth. A spacecraft designed for the inner solar system would need a fundamentally different thermal design to operate in the outer solar system, and the thermal test program must reflect the actual mission environment.

2. What Thermal-Vacuum Testing Must Achieve

Thermal-vacuum testing must accomplish several distinct objectives, each requiring a different test configuration and test sequence. Understanding these objectives — and ensuring the test program is designed to address each of them — is the starting point for T-V test planning.

  • Thermal model correlation: The spacecraft's predicted temperature distribution during T-V testing must be compared to measured temperatures from the test. Significant discrepancies indicate errors in the thermal mathematical model (TMM) that must be understood and corrected. The correlated TMM is then used to predict on-orbit temperatures with confidence, including the cases the chamber cannot reproduce. A T-V test that does not allow adequate thermal model correlation is a missed opportunity of the highest order. Section 5 describes what happens when the model and the test do not agree.
  • Verification of thermal design margins: Every component on the spacecraft has a defined allowable flight temperature range. T-V testing must demonstrate that the thermal design holds every component within its allowable range under the hot and cold extremes of the mission thermal environment — not just at nominal operating conditions.
  • Functional performance verification across the thermal range: Hardware that passes functional tests at ambient temperature may not perform correctly at the cold end of its operating range, or may fail in subtle ways at the hot end. T-V testing provides the temperature-controlled environment within which functional verification is performed at temperature extremes.
  • Workmanship screening at temperature: Thermal cycling in vacuum exposes marginal workmanship defects — particularly solder joint fatigue, adhesive bond failures, and connector interface degradation — that may not be revealed by ambient-temperature functional testing or by the vibration test program.
  • Outgassing verification: Materials are screened for outgassing as individual samples before they are approved for flight, but the assembled spacecraft is not the same thing as its approved materials list. Residual solvents, handling contamination, incompletely cured adhesives and potting, and the sheer quantity of material in an integrated vehicle all contribute volatiles that sample-level screening cannot predict. T-V testing drives these off in a controlled environment, where chamber cold surfaces and contamination monitors capture and characterize them, rather than leaving them to outgas on orbit where they can deposit on optics and thermal control surfaces.
  • Heater circuit verification: Spacecraft thermal control heaters keep components above their cold limits, and T-V testing is where they are verified under the conditions they were designed for. The obvious failure is a heater that fails open and allows hardware to cool below its survival limit. Two others matter as much. A thermostat that fails closed leaves its heater stuck on and can overheat the component it protects. Wiring and mapping errors are the defects T-V testing most often actually finds: a heater wired to the wrong zone, a thermostat or thermistor controlling a different heater than the drawings say, or primary and redundant circuits crossed. These errors can pass ambient functional testing entirely, because nothing is cold enough to make them visible. Verifying that each heater turns on, at the right setpoint, in the right place, and with the right sensor responding is a critical safety function of the cold phases of the test.
  • Verification of the as-built spacecraft: A sound thermal design does not guarantee a spacecraft that matches it.. T-V testing shows whether thermal coatings and blankets were manufactured and installed correctly, and whether the internal temperature sensors work and are installed where the drawings say. It also shows whether performance changes unexpectedly with temperature, such as an oscillator drifting in frequency (in or out of specification) or a mechanism moving faster or slower than it did at ambient. It shows whether temperature-based safety limits inhibit or permit operation when they should not. And it exposes infant-mortality failures and latent defects in units that passed testing before they became part of the larger assembly. These findings can be manufacturing, quality, assembly, or even software issues rather than design flaws. They lead to investigation and repair, or to changed operating parameters such as telemetry limits.
  • Operations familiarization: The test lets the flight operations team see the spacecraft in action and learn what they are likely to see on orbit, in at least some operating modes.

Each of these objectives has an owner. Correlation belongs to the thermal analysts, margin verification and heater performance to thermal engineering, functional performance to the subsystem and systems engineers, as-built findings to quality assurance and the subsystem engineers, operations familiarization to the flight operations team, and outgassing to contamination engineering. None of them is optional, and each owner has an estimate of the chamber time and the spacecraft thermal state their objective needs. The I&T lead's job is to fold those estimates into one plan, overlapping activities wherever possible so every objective is completed and verified as efficiently as the chamber allows, without one group's activity interfering with another's. Flight operations cannot run the spacecraft during a cold soak, for example, but they can during a transition. Operating during a cold-to-hot transition adds dissipation and shortens it. Operating during a hot-to-cold transition slows it, at a cost in chamber time, but gives the operations team experience of how the spacecraft behaves as it will in flight. Because these are estimates, the schedule and staffing are built on them with margin, knowing that shifts, staffing and the operations timeline may change. The plan is fixed before the test starts and becomes dynamic once the chamber closes, which is why it needs to be built in a planning meeting, not worked out during week three. When time runs short, the I&T lead brings options to systems engineering and project management, drawing on the team's expertise and on experience from other programs: what can be overlapped, what can be resequenced, what each choice costs. Whether to accept the risk of shortening or dropping an objective is their call. Once they make it, the I&T lead implements it.

3. The Thermal-Vacuum Chamber

A thermal-vacuum chamber is a pressure vessel from which the air is evacuated to simulate the vacuum of space, fitted with internal surfaces that can be cooled to deep-space temperatures or heated to simulate solar loading. The hardware that a spacecraft encounters in its test chamber — cold walls, solar simulators, thermal shrouds — determines the fidelity with which the space thermal environment is replicated on the ground.

Thermal-Vacuum Chamber — Principal Systems
Environmental Systems
Vacuum Turbomolecular and cryogenic pumps achieve pressures of 10⁻⁵ to 10⁻⁷ Torr — high vacuum that effectively eliminates convective heat transfer and replicates the gaseous environment of space
Cold Wall Thermal shrouds lining the chamber, cooled with liquid nitrogen (LN₂) to approximately −196°C to act as a heat sink, accepting radiated heat from the test article as space does in flight. Many facilities can also run the shrouds on temperature-controlled gaseous nitrogen across a wide range, which is often how thermal transitions and cycling are driven. Which mode the facility uses affects transition times and therefore the test schedule.
Solar Sim High-power xenon or quartz-tungsten-halogen lamps with spectral filtering that approximate the solar spectrum at the required flux level. Used for thermal balance testing that requires simulated solar illumination
Test Heaters Infrared lamp arrays, heater plates, and test heaters attached directly to the spacecraft or its radiators, providing controlled heat input without solar-simulator spectral fidelity. These are the most common way of producing hot and cold case conditions. Test heaters add their own power, control, and feedthrough requirements, and they must be removed or verified safe before flight.
Test Support Systems
EGSE Feed Vacuum-rated electrical feedthroughs that carry power, commands, and telemetry between the spacecraft inside the chamber and the EGSE outside. Must handle all spacecraft interfaces without introducing ground loops, electrical noise, thermal shorts or contamination paths
RF Feeds RF-transparent windows or vacuum feedthroughs for spacecraft communications and transponder testing. Allows end-to-end RF testing through the chamber wall while under vacuum
LN₂ Supply Continuous liquid nitrogen supply system maintaining shroud temperatures. A large T-V test may consume thousands of liters of LN₂ per day — supply reliability is a critical facility risk
Contamination Cold fingers, witness plates and mirrors, QCM sensors, an RGA (residual gas analyzer), and other detectors such as surface acoustic wave (SAW) sensors monitor outgassing and contamination before and throughout the test. Contamination events are documented and may trigger test holds
✦ Chamber Size and the Scale Problem

The single most common constraint on spacecraft T-V testing is chamber size. For small to medium spacecraft, suitable facilities are widely available. For large observatories, the available chambers are few, heavily booked, and enormously expensive to operate. The James Webb Space Telescope is the extreme case. Its telescope and instrument module was tested in Chamber A at NASA's Johnson Space Center, a facility originally built for Apollo, with a working volume inside its nitrogen shroud of 16.8 meters in diameter and 27.4 meters tall. Chamber A required extensive modification, including new helium-cooled shrouds, to reach the deep cryogenic temperatures the test demanded, because no existing facility of adequate size could do it as built.

Programs building large spacecraft must plan for their T-V facility requirements years in advance. Chamber availability and size have delayed programs and driven test compromises, such as separate thermal-vacuum tests of the spacecraft bus and the instrument or telescope module. JWST was tested this way because no chamber could hold the whole observatory: the telescope and instrument module (OTIS) was tested in Chamber A at Johnson Space Center, with the heat from the spacecraft bus simulated by ground equipment, and the spacecraft element (bus and sunshield) was tested separately at Northrop Grumman. A split campaign introduces additional risks and requires careful analysis to show that the separate tests together constitute adequate system-level verification, including at the interfaces neither test fully exercises.

The Chamber Is a Cleanroom

A thermal-vacuum chamber is treated and operated as a cleanroom, and its cleanliness is part of the test. Preparing it is the same work as preparing any cleanroom for integration: controlling who and what enters, gowning, cleaning, monitoring, and documenting, often with the same people and procedures. A chamber large enough for personnel to enter requires the same gowning and access control as a cleanroom or clean tent, and the entrance where the hardware goes in is often a clean tent, so the spacecraft moves from the integration cleanroom to the chamber without being exposed to uncontrolled air. The difference is the materials and the consequences. Everything inside must survive vacuum and temperature extremes without outgassing, so items that are routine in an ordinary cleanroom, such as certain tapes, adhesives, lubricants, and plastics, may be rejected here, and a lapse that would be a wipe-down in a cleanroom can become a film on optics or radiators once the chamber is cold. The cleanroom article earlier in this series covers the underlying protocols.

The chamber is prepared before the spacecraft arrives. It is typically run empty first, which verifies that the vacuum, shroud, control, and data systems work and also cleans and bakes out the chamber so its cleanliness can be certified. It may then be run again with the test cables and instrumentation that will be used in the test inside it, to clean them. Alternatively, those items are baked out in a separate chamber and moved in afterward. Throughout, the chamber is monitored with cold fingers (cooled surfaces that collect condensable material for later analysis), witness plates, quartz crystal microbalances, residual gas analyzers, and other detectors such as SAW sensors. Contamination engineering reviews the results against the program's cleanliness criteria before the spacecraft moves in.

There are a lot of preparations required leading up to installing the spacecraft into the chamber. Each has an owner. The I&T lead is involved in all of them, wheter simply making sure each one is actually moving, to leading or directing certain activitits. At a minimum, they ensure that the paperwork, work orders, and facility requests exist and are being worked, not sitting in someone's queue, and that the owners get the help, scheduling, and tracking they need:

  • Chamber history. What was tested last, and how the chamber was cleaned afterward, affects how much cleaning it needs now. Facility and contamination engineering own the answer.
  • Chamber certification. The empty-chamber and cable-cleaning runs happen before the spacecraft arrives and add days to weeks. They need their own slot in the facility schedule, and contamination control needs to be on track to certify the chamber before the hardware moves.
  • Everything that goes in. Fixtures, MGSE, tapes, adhesives, cable ties, thermocouple mounting materials, and test blankets are reviewed by contamination engineering, usually against outgassing data, before they enter. The teams supplying them need to get their lists in early enough for that review.
  • Cold surfaces are the sinks. Whatever outgasses tends to condense on the coldest surfaces it can reach. That includes the shrouds, and it also includes the spacecraft's own cold radiators, optics, and detectors. Covers, purges, and the bake-out sequence all exist to keep contamination off the second group, and they have to be planned and in place before the door closes.
  • Pumping and backfill. The chamber must not become the source. Backstreaming from the pumps is controlled, and repressurization uses filtered dry nitrogen (see Section 7). The facility owns this, and the I&T lead confirms it is in the procedure.
  • Test plans and criteria. The thermal team produces the test plan, the temperature criteria, and the plateau and stability definitions. Everything else in the campaign depends on them, so their delivery dates belong on the schedule.
  • Test support infrastructure and wiring. Fixtures, shroud interfaces, test cabling, feedthrough wiring, heaters, and thermocouple installation come from the mechanical, thermal, and facility teams, and they have to arrive on time and be verified before closeout.
  • After the test. Witness plates and samples are analyzed, and the chamber is returned to cleanroom condition, with the contamination record kept alongside the test data.

The I&T lead's job across all of this is to keep it moving: tracking each item to its owner, helping clear obstacles, scheduling around dependencies, and raising problems while there is still time to fix them. The campaign is not ready until every one of them is done.

4. Temperature Limit Definitions

The thermal design and test program is organized around a hierarchy of temperature limits for each component. Those limits drive the thermal portion of every T-V procedure. They set the temperature each plateau must reach, how long the spacecraft dwells there, the criteria for moving on to the next step, and the points at which a reading calls for serious action or an anomaly investigation. Because the limit types are easy to confuse, applying the wrong one to a reading can cause an anomaly during the test, or make it look like one. That is why the limits are worked out and defined carefully before the test, entered correctly into the procedures and the test software, and double-checked as part of test preparation.

Tier What It Means How It Is Set Role in the T-V Test
Predicted Flight Temperatures The hottest and coldest temperatures each component is expected to see over the mission Thermal mathematical model, with the uncertainty margin required by the program's thermal design rules The baseline that every test temperature is measured from. Updated after correlation.
Acceptance Test Temperatures The range previously qualified flight hardware is tested over to screen workmanship Predicted extremes plus an acceptance margin set by the governing standard (GEVS: ±5°C for thermal-vacuum, versus ±10°C for qualification and protoflight) Plateau targets for acceptance T-V testing
Qualification / Protoflight Test Temperatures The range over which the design is proven to work, with margin beyond anything expected in flight Predicted extremes ±10°C under NASA and GEVS practice, unless the mission specification says otherwise Plateau targets for qualification and protoflight T-V testing; the range functional testing must pass across
Survival (Non-Operating) Limits The range the hardware can tolerate unpowered without permanent damage Unit qualification and design data Hard limits for unpowered phases and for facility-failure contingencies; never to be exceeded

A note on sources: the margins above are taken from the Goddard Environmental Verification Standard (GSFC-STD-7000B, April 2021, Table 2.2-2). GEVS is the basis for many test parameters across the industry, but it is not universal. Other organizations, such as JPL, ESA, and DoD programs, maintain their own standards, and those may or may not have GEVS as their basis or origin. Always apply the standard your program is governed by.

Programs use different names for these tiers. JPL and many other organizations frame margins around an allowable flight temperature (AFT), while GSFC practice frames them relative to predicted temperatures. The I&T lead's job is not to referee the terminology. It is to make sure every procedure, redline table, and monitoring display on the program uses one set of definitions, consistently, and that everyone on every shift knows which limit applies to which sensor.

The First Component to Hit Its Limit

At system level, the spacecraft cannot be driven to every component's test temperature at once. Heat flows through the whole vehicle, so as the chamber pushes toward a hot or cold plateau, some component will reach its test limit first. When it does, the rest of the spacecraft stops where it is, short of its own target. The thermal team then has to decide whether to accept the shortfall, apply local test heaters or cooling to drive the lagging components further, or verify their margin some other way. Each option has schedule, configuration, and feedthrough consequences. The I&T lead needs to know before the test which components are expected to be test-limiting, and what the plan is when they are.

The margins between these tiers exist for a reason. They account for thermal model uncertainty, unit-to-unit variation, and degradation of thermal control hardware over the mission lifetime. Programs under thermal design pressure sometimes propose to reduce them. Any margin reduction must be formally justified and approved. The margin is not contingency reserve to be spent without an engineering assessment of the consequences.

5. Thermal Balance Testing

A thermal balance test (TBT) is the component of the T-V test program that specifically aims to validate the spacecraft's thermal mathematical model. The test establishes controlled steady-state thermal conditions that correspond to defined mission thermal environments — typically the hot case (maximum solar input, maximum internal power dissipation) and the cold case (minimum solar input, minimum internal power dissipation, eclipse) — and measures the resulting temperature distribution across the spacecraft.

The measured temperatures are then compared to the predictions of the thermal mathematical model for the same test conditions. Agreement between test and model validates the model's accuracy. Discrepancies — and there will always be some — must be investigated and explained. The investigation may reveal errors in material property inputs (surface emissivities, absorptivities, thermal conductivities), incorrect boundary conditions in the model, or unanticipated heat transfer paths within the spacecraft structure.

Test Cases: Hot and Cold

A minimum of two thermal balance test cases is required for most spacecraft: the hot case and the cold case. These correspond to the boundary conditions that produce the highest and lowest component temperatures in the mission thermal environment.

  • Hot case: Combines the conditions that drive temperatures highest. These are maximum solar flux (for Earth orbiters, near perihelion in early January), the orbit geometry with the least eclipse and the most sunlight on the critical surfaces, end-of-life thermal coating properties (solar absorptance increases as coatings degrade), and maximum internal power dissipation. The hot case verifies that the thermal control system can reject enough heat to keep every component below its hot limits.
  • Cold case: Combines the conditions that drive temperatures lowest. These are minimum solar flux (near aphelion in early July for Earth orbiters), maximum eclipse duration, beginning-of-life coating properties, and minimum internal power dissipation, often survival or safe mode. The cold case verifies that heaters and passive thermal control keep every component above its cold limits, and it is often the limiting case for batteries and propulsion components.
  • Orbital average / transition cases: For complex missions with rapidly varying thermal environments — spinning spacecraft, instruments with cryogenic coolers, or missions with significant orbital eccentricity — additional test cases may be required to capture transient thermal conditions not represented by the steady-state hot and cold cases.

How Long Is a Balance Plateau?

A thermal balance case is complete when the spacecraft reaches steady state, defined in the test plan as a stability criterion: temperatures at specified locations changing by less than a set rate over a set period. The spacecraft decides how long that takes, not the schedule. Thermally massive structures, components with large thermal time constants, and anything heavily insulated can take a long time to settle, and the slowest node sets the pace for the whole case. The I&T lead should know from the thermal team's pre-test transient predictions which nodes are expected to be slowest and how long each balance case is likely to take, and should treat those durations as estimates with real uncertainty rather than fixed timeline entries.

⚙ When Test and Model Disagree

Thermal model correlation is an iterative engineering process, not a single comparison. The first comparison between test data and model predictions almost always reveals discrepancies, and the program's correlation criteria, set by the thermal team, define which ones must be explained before the model can be trusted for flight predictions.

For the I&T lead, a significant discrepancy is a campaign event, not just an analysis task. The thermal team may ask to extend a balance plateau to collect more data, to add a test case the plan did not include, or to change a boundary condition and re-establish steady state. Every one of those requests consumes chamber time. Some discrepancies turn out to be test-setup problems rather than model errors, such as a misplaced or debonded thermocouple, an unexpected heat leak through test cabling, or MGSE that is not behaving as modeled. Those can sometimes be resolved only by changing the configuration, which may mean breaking vacuum.

The questions to ask are how much time the request needs, what it displaces, whether the question can be answered later from data already collected, and what risk the program accepts if it cannot. The correlated model is used for every thermal prediction after launch, so time spent here is rarely wasted. But it has to be spent deliberately.

What a Failed Correlation Can Cost

A thermal model that will not correlate is not automatically a hardware problem, but it forces a decision. The possible outcomes run from cheap to expensive:

  • Fix the model. Most discrepancies end here: a contact conductance, the effective emittance of the multi-layer insulation, or an unmodeled heat path. The on-orbit predictions are re-run, and if flight temperatures still fall inside limits with margin, nothing on the spacecraft changes. The question to ask is whether the cause is understood, or whether the model was simply adjusted until it matched.
  • Change flight settings. Heater setpoints, thermostat or control-table values, and telemetry limits can often be changed without touching hardware, but a colder-than-predicted spacecraft may need heater power the budget does not have.
  • Change operations. Sun-angle and attitude constraints, time limits in a given attitude, instrument warm-up and duty cycles, eclipse-season procedures, and safe-mode thermal behavior can all absorb a discrepancy. This costs no hardware, but it costs mission flexibility, and the flight operations team lives with the result for the life of the mission.
  • Change hardware. Heater size or count, MLI, radiator area or coatings, thermal straps, doublers, and interface fillers are all candidates. Each means breaking vacuum, reworking the hardware, and running a functional test of whatever was touched, often with another chamber entry and a mass or power penalty.
  • Retest. Repeating or extending a balance phase, or scheduling a second chamber entry, costs a chamber slot, a full crew, and weeks of schedule.
  • Accept the risk. A waiver or reduced margin, paired with an on-orbit verification plan and a defined fallback during commissioning.

Every rung above the second becomes far more expensive once the chamber is warm and the vacuum is broken. That is why a preliminary correlation belongs inside the test, while the spacecraft is still cold and the data can still change the plan, and why the decision on which outcome to pursue should have an owner named before the chamber closes.

6. Thermal Cycling and Thermal-Vacuum Cycling

While thermal balance testing validates the thermal model, thermal cycling is the primary workmanship screen in the thermal domain. Thermal cycling subjects the hardware to repeated transitions between temperature extremes, exposing marginal solder joints, bonded interfaces, and material interfaces to the differential thermal expansion stresses that will act on them in flight.

Two variants are used on spacecraft programs:

  • Thermal-vacuum cycling: Cycling between the hot and cold test temperatures inside a vacuum chamber, performed at the component level and again at the system level. It combines workmanship screening with functional verification at temperature in a representative environment, and it is the primary thermal verification test at both levels.
  • Thermal cycling at ambient pressure: Cycling in a thermal chamber with air or nitrogen, typically at the component or board level. Convection allows much faster temperature changes and more cycles in less time, which makes it an efficient workmanship screen. It does not represent the vacuum environment, so it supplements thermal-vacuum testing rather than replacing it, and standards that allow it usually require additional cycles in compensation.

Cycle Counts and Transition Rates

The minimum number of cycles for each level of assembly is set by the governing standard, such as GEVS or the mission's environmental verification specification. These minimums are workmanship screens, based on long experience of how much cycling it takes to precipitate latent defects. Fatigue life is a separate question, addressed by design and packaging qualification. It matters most for missions in low Earth orbit that accumulate thousands of thermal cycles per year, and it also bounds the test from the other direction: the screening must not consume a meaningful fraction of the hardware's life.

Transition rates differ sharply between levels of assembly. A component in a small chamber can be ramped at a controlled rate of a few degrees per minute. A full spacecraft changes temperature only as fast as its thermal mass and the chamber shrouds allow, which is usually far slower. That is a major reason a system-level campaign takes weeks rather than days. At system level, the rate that matters is usually the maximum allowed for sensitive hardware such as optics, batteries, and adhesively bonded assemblies, where gradients during a fast transition can exceed anything seen in flight.

⚠ The Cycle Count Temptation

Under schedule pressure, programs frequently look at the remaining thermal cycles as a source of time. "We've already done six; do we really need eight?" Each cycle at system level can cost the better part of a day or more, so the temptation is real, and it usually arrives late in the campaign when everyone is tired and the launch date is fixed.

The answer is that the cycle count is a verification requirement, not a planning estimate. Stopping short of it means the workmanship screen the program committed to was not performed. That is a waiver, and it requires the same formal risk assessment and approval as any other waiver. It should not be an informal decision made in the control room. The I&T lead's job is to make sure the decision is made at the right level, with the risk stated plainly, and to point out the cheaper alternative: build the cycles into the schedule honestly at the start, so the question never has to be asked.

7. The Thermal-Vacuum Test Campaign: Phases and Sequence

A complete system-level T-V test campaign is a multi-week operation that combines thermal balance testing, survival exposures, thermal cycling, functional testing, and anomaly resolution in a single continuous sequence inside the vacuum chamber. Programs order these elements differently. Balance cases may come before or after cycling, or be folded into the first cycle, and survival exposures may be combined with cycling plateaus. A bake-out (Section 11) is often added as the final hot phase, just before warm-up and repressurization.

Pre-Test Functional Baseline
A comprehensive performance test at ambient temperature and pressure, performed in the final test configuration before the chamber is sealed. It establishes the baseline against which all in-vacuum and post-test results are compared. Any anomaly found here must be resolved before the door closes, because investigation in vacuum is severely constrained by the inability to access the hardware.

PUMPDOWN
Chamber Evacuation (Pumpdown)
The chamber is pumped down over hours to days, depending on chamber size and how much the spacecraft outgasses. A functional test at vacuum but near ambient temperature, before any thermal stress is applied. That way, effects of vacuum are not later confused with effects of temperature.
🔥
HOT BAL
Hot Case Thermal Balance
The spacecraft is brought to the hot balance case conditions using internal dissipation and test heaters or solar simulation, and held until the stability criterion in the test plan is met. That can take a day or more. Temperatures are recorded for model correlation, and the case is held until the thermal team confirms the data are sufficient. Correlation itself continues after the test.
❄️
COLD BAL
Cold Case Thermal Balance
The same process at the cold case conditions. The cold case exercises heater circuits, thermostat setpoints, and the conductance of thermal straps and heat pipes under the conditions they were designed for. It is often the limiting case for batteries, propulsion lines, and attitude control electronics.
🧊
COLD START
Survival Exposure and Cold Turn-On
With the spacecraft or selected units unpowered, the hardware is driven toward its survival limits, then powered on to demonstrate that it starts and operates correctly from that condition. This is the ground counterpart of safe-hold, the mode a spacecraft enters when the onboard computer detects certain faults. Safe-hold details differ by mission, but the idea is the same everywhere: turn off everything except the minimum needed to recover. With most equipment off, little heat is generated, and in the worst case the spacecraft is very cold and still getting colder. The test shows that the heaters that stay powered hold the hardware above its survival limits, and that the recovery equipment starts from the coldest condition it could see in flight. A hot turn-on is often demonstrated as well. These are among the highest-risk events in the campaign. Units are deliberately taken outside their operating range, so redlines, abort criteria, and recovery steps must be explicit in the procedure, and the right engineers must be in the room when power is applied.
🔄
CYCLING
Thermal-Vacuum Cycling
The number of cycles required by the governing standard is performed between the hot and cold test temperatures. Functional testing is performed at the plateaus of each cycle, not just at the end, so that any degradation can be tied to a specific cycle and temperature. Anomalies are characterized by whether they appear at one extreme, both, during transitions, or only after repeated cycles. Each of those patterns points to a different kind of cause.
✅
POST-VAC
Post-Cycling Vacuum Functional Test
A comprehensive performance test at ambient temperature, still in vacuum, after all thermal exposures are complete. It is compared directly with the pre-cycling vacuum CPT test. Any degradation must be investigated before the chamber is vented, while the test conditions still exist.
🌬️
REPRESS
Warm-Up and Repressurization
Before venting, the spacecraft is brought above the dew point of the backfill gas everywhere, including cold-biased components and the chamber's cold surfaces. Otherwise, moisture and contaminants condense onto flight hardware. The chamber is then backfilled, normally with dry nitrogen, at a controlled rate. As in pumpdown, high-voltage equipment stays off while the pressure passes through the critical range. Repressurization is a contamination event in its own right, and contamination engineering should agree the procedure in advance.
📋
POST-TEST
Post-Test Ambient Functional Test
A final comprehensive performance test at ambient conditions, compared against both the pre-test baseline and the post-cycling vacuum results. Post-test inspection of thermal coatings, MLI, bonded items, and test instrumentation follows. The post-test CPT test is the formal record of the spacecraft's condition at the end of the campaign.

8. Running the Campaign: The I&T Lead's Side of Thermal-Vacuum

Section 2 listed the objectives and who owns them. The I&T lead is responsible for building a campaign that can deliver all of them: configured correctly, staffed around the clock, protected against facility failures, and prepared for the anomaly that will inevitably appear at the least convenient moment.

Before the Door Closes

Planning and preparing for a thermal-vacuum test often takes two to three times as long or longer as for a vibration or EMC test, and the reasons are built into the test. It is expensive to run, and expensive and time-consuming to stop. Once the chamber is sealed, the spacecraft cannot be touched. And nearly every discipline has an objective in the test and something to prepare for it. Every omission found after pumpdown is therefore costly. The readiness checklist covers several items:

It is expensive to run, and expensive and time-consuming to stop. Once the chamber is sealed, the spacecraft cannot be touched. And nearly every discipline has an objective in the test and something to prepare for it. Every omission found after pumpdown is therefore costly. The readiness checklist has two parts:

The plan and the people

  • Every objective has a named owner, an agreed time estimate, and defined success criteria.
  • The integrated test timeline is approved, showing which activities overlap, which need the chamber to themselves, and how much margin it carries.
  • The plateau-by-plateau functional test matrix is approved.
  • Flight operations procedures and personnel are ready for the windows assigned to them.
  • The shift schedule, test conductor roster, and on-call list, with backups, are in place.
  • The anomaly response plan is in place, including the break-vacuum criteria and who makes the call.

The hardware and the facility

  • The as-built configuration is documented before closeout, including blanket and coating inspection and photographs, so post-test findings have a baseline to compare against.
  • The thermocouple installation is closed out and verified channel-by-channel against the map.
  • Feedthrough pin-outs are checked end to end.
  • Thermal data reaches the analysts in near real time, so a preliminary correlation can happen during the test.
  • Calibration, certification, and maintenance dates for all test and facility equipment are verified valid through the planned test end date plus schedule margin.
  • Redlines are defined for both spacecraft and facility parameters.

The pre-test functional baseline described in Section 7 is the last opportunity to find a problem while the hardware can still be touched.

Certifications and Maintenance That Must Outlast the Test

Article 05 covers the test equipment calibration program in detail, but thermal-vacuum testing puts a particular strain on it. Here the requirement is not just that equipment is calibrated and certified when the test starts. Every calibration and certification has to remain valid until the test ends, with margin for the delays that a multi-week campaign almost always experiences. An instrument or system whose certification expires mid-test cannot simply be swapped out behind a sealed chamber door. Depending on what it is, the expiration can halt the test, invalidate the data taken with it, or force a choice between breaking vacuum and running on equipment that is no longer certified.

Satisfying that requirement means looking forward. Calibration and certification dates for everything used in the test are checked against the planned end date plus a reasonable allowance for slips. Anything that would expire inside that window is recalibrated or recertified early, even if that means doing it out of its normal cycle. The scope covers EGSE and test instrumentation, the facility data system and its sensors, pressure and vacuum gauges, contamination monitors, cryogen systems, and the lifting equipment used to move the spacecraft into and out of the chamber.

The same logic applies to routine and preventive maintenance on the chamber and its support systems. That includes vacuum pumps, shroud and nitrogen systems, facility power and backup power, and anything else the test depends on. Maintenance that would normally fall due during the test window needs to be identified and, where possible, performed before the campaign begins, rather than discovered as a facility outage in week three.

Only some of this is the I&T lead's direct responsibility. Much of it belongs to the facility, metrology, safety, and ground systems organizations. Confirming that it has all been done, however, is squarely the I&T lead's job. That means checking with each responsible organization, verifying that the necessary work has actually been completed, and making sure any delay in completing it is identified early and factored into the test readiness schedule. A test that is technically ready but waiting on a recalibration is not ready.

Getting Signals Through the Wall

The chamber wall is the most important interface in the campaign. The feedthroughs must carry every spacecraft interface plus test heater power, facility instrumentation, and test thermocouples. The test cables inside the chamber must be vacuum-compatible and low-outgassing, and they must be routed so they do not add unmodeled heat leaks to the spacecraft. The EGSE outside the chamber now has to run for weeks without unplanned downtime, so redundancy and spares for ground equipment become schedule-critical in a way they are not in the cleanroom. Time-tagging between spacecraft telemetry and the facility data system must be synchronized. Otherwise, correlating a temperature excursion with a spacecraft event becomes an exercise in guesswork.

Staffing a 24/7 Campaign

A multi-week round-the-clock test needs a deliberate shift structure: a test conductor on every shift, defined roles for operators and discipline engineers, and an on-call list for the engineers who are not in the room. Shift handovers are where information gets lost. A formal handover covering spacecraft state, the current plateau, open anomalies, and upcoming transitions is as much a test control as any procedure. The on-call list should name a person and a backup for every discipline that owns an objective in section 2, so that no question or anomaly waits on someone who cannot be reached.

The Human Side of the Campaign

One of the biggest challenges in a thermal-vacuum campaign is not technical. It is human. For weeks on end, everyone supporting the test is asked to be present, observant, and effective the entire time. Shifts may run 8, 10, or 12 hours, for 5 to 7 days in a row, before anyone gets a break, and the work goes on around the clock. People become fatigued and sleep-deprived. Some start to get sick. They still have lives outside the test: spouses, children, obligations at home, and personal problems that do not pause because the chamber is running. All of it wears on concentration, judgment, mood, teamwork, and civility. Those are the qualities the test depends on most, and they erode most quickly when the campaign runs long and an anomaly arrives in the middle of the night.

It is not the I&T lead's job to solve every one of those problems. It is the I&T lead's responsibility to plan for them. That means shift schedules people can sustain for the length of the campaign, not just for the first week. It means making sure people actually take their breaks and days off, rather than treating breaks as optional when the schedule is tight. And it means building enough depth into the staffing plan that the test does not depend on any one person being there. When someone gets sick, has a family emergency, or simply reaches the point where they are no longer safe to be making decisions about flight hardware, there has to be a qualified person ready to step in. A staffing plan with no slack in it is not a plan. It is a bet that nothing will go wrong with the people, made in a campaign designed around the certainty that something will.

When Something Goes Wrong in Vacuum

An anomaly in the chamber cannot be investigated the way it would be in the cleanroom. The options are limited:

  • Troubleshoot through telemetry and commanding.
  • Adjust the test sequence.
  • Continue with a documented workaround.
  • Return to ambient and vent.

Breaking vacuum typically costs days of schedule, between warm-up, repressurization, access, and the pumpdown and thermal stabilization needed to resume. It may also invalidate test conditions already achieved. That is why the criteria for breaking vacuum should be agreed among the project, thermal, and I&T before pumpdown, when the decision can be made on engineering grounds rather than under schedule pressure. Choosing among the options is a risk decision, not an I&T decision. Continuing with a workaround, skipping a planned test step, or returning to ambient early all trade schedule against risk, and that trade belongs to systems engineering and project management, with the discipline leads advising. The I&T lead's role is to lay out the options and their costs quickly, recommend one, and carry out whichever the project chooses.

The Facility Is Part of the Test

Facility failures are a spacecraft risk. These include interruption of the LN₂ supply, power outages, pump failures, and loss of chamber control. Each credible facility failure needs a defined spacecraft response: safing commands, heater configurations, and the conditions under which the spacecraft is powered down. Those responses must be rehearsed with the facility operators before the test, not worked out the first time an alarm sounds.

9. Thermal Failure Modes — What T-V Testing Reveals

Each failure mode below is a reason for a particular measurement, redline, functional test, or plateau to be in the procedure. Knowing what the test is trying to catch at each temperature extreme is what lets the I&T lead judge which parts of the test sequence can be compressed under pressure and which cannot.

❄️
Cold-Case Failures
Below operating temperature

Battery performance and charging: Lithium-ion and NiCd batteries lose capacity and voltage as temperature falls, and a battery that stays within its survival limit can still deliver too little voltage to carry peak loads. Cold also constrains charging: lithium-ion cells charged at low temperature can suffer lithium plating and permanent capacity loss. Battery charge management during the cold phases is an operational constraint the procedure must respect, not just a design margin to verify. Battery thermal control is the most common cold-case design driver.

Lubricant solidification: Mechanism lubricants that are fluid at room temperature can become highly viscous or solidify at cold operating temperatures, causing mechanism stall or excessive bearing torque. Reaction wheels, deployment mechanisms, and propellant flow control valves are all cold-temperature-sensitive.

Oscillator frequency shift: Crystal oscillators experience frequency shifts with temperature; cold-temperature shifts may take the frequency outside the allowable tolerance for the communications system or for onboard timing references.

Heater circuit failure: A heater that fails to turn on during a cold plateau is one of the most serious findings in a T-V campaign. Many heater circuits have no individual current telemetry, so a failure usually shows up indirectly, as a zone that keeps cooling past its setpoint, or as bus current that does not step up when the heater should have switched on. Combined with the wiring and mapping errors described in Section 2, this is why heater verification relies on watching temperature response zone by zone rather than assuming a heater works because it was commanded.

🔥
Hot-Case Failures
Above operating temperature

Thermal runaway in electronics: Some electronic components, particularly voltage regulators and power transistors, have positive thermal feedback: as temperature rises, dissipation rises, which raises temperature further. Without adequate heat sinking, this can run away to permanent failure. Derating analysis and unit-level testing carry most of the burden of preventing it, and hot-case system testing is the final check that heat sinking holds up in the integrated vehicle.

Exceeding junction temperature limits: Electronic component manufacturers specify maximum junction temperatures — the temperature at the semiconductor junction, which can be tens of degrees above the case temperature for high-dissipation devices. Junction temperatures are typically measured by inference from case temperatures plus a dissipation-based calculation; hot-case testing is required to verify that junction temperatures remain within specification under worst-case conditions.

Thermal interface degradation: Thermal interface materials — thermally conductive pads, phase-change materials, indium foils — that appeared adequate at room temperature may provide insufficient conductance at high temperatures if they have not seated properly or if contact pressure has been reduced by differential thermal expansion. Hot-case testing reveals inadequate thermal interfaces that will allow component temperatures to exceed predictions.

Battery degradation at high temperature: Batteries often have the narrowest allowable temperature band on the spacecraft, and the upper end of it matters as much as the lower. Elevated temperature accelerates capacity fade and aging in lithium-ion cells, so a battery that functions perfectly in the hot case may still be losing life faster than the mission can afford. Hot-case testing verifies that the battery's thermal design, including its radiator and its isolation from hot neighbors, keeps it within that band at maximum dissipation.

🔄
Thermal Cycling Failures
Fatigue and differential expansion

Solder joint fatigue: The most common failure mode revealed by thermal cycling. Differential thermal expansion between electronic component bodies and PCB substrates generates shear stress at solder joints with each temperature cycle. Marginal joints — those with insufficient solder volume, poor wetting, or voided bonds — accumulate fatigue damage and eventually crack. This failure mode is the primary justification for thermal cycling as a workmanship screen.

Adhesive bond failures: Bonded structural joints — optical mounts, heat pipe interface bonds, component potting — are subject to peel and shear stresses from differential thermal expansion. Bonds with inadequate coverage, voids, or insufficient adhesive modulus for the thermal cycling range can crack or delaminate during cycling, producing misalignment or loss of thermal contact.

Connector interface degradation: Connector contact resistance can increase with repeated thermal cycling as contact finishes degrade and fretting wear occurs at the contact interface. Intermittent connections that are stable at ambient temperature may open or develop high resistance during thermal cycling — often manifesting as performance degradation that is difficult to reproduce after the temperature is returned to ambient.

Coatings and surface finish delamination: Thermal control coatings — white paints, black anodizes, second-surface mirrors — can crack, blister, or delaminate under repeated thermal cycling. Coating failures change the surface optical properties and can invalidate the thermal design basis used for the TMM. Visual inspection after cycling is essential to detect coating anomalies before closeout.

⚠ Not Every Failure Comes From the Spacecraft

A T-V test can damage the hardware it is meant to verify. The most common causes are a test heater whose control fails and drives hardware past its limits, a shroud or facility excursion beyond the planned temperatures, test instrumentation that damages thermal coatings or optical surfaces when installed or removed, and test cabling that creates a heat path the thermal design never anticipated. These are test configuration problems, and they belong to the I&T lead. Independent overtemperature protection on test heaters, facility redlines tied to the spacecraft's limits rather than the chamber's, and a careful instrumentation removal plan are part of the test design, not afterthoughts.

10. Instrumentation and Data Interpretation

The engineering value of a T-V test is entirely dependent on the quality and density of its temperature instrumentation. A test with inadequate thermocouple coverage may achieve the required thermal plateau and complete all functional tests without providing the data needed to correlate the thermal model or to support future anomaly investigations.

🌡️
Thermocouples (TCs)
The workhorse of T-V instrumentation. Type T (copper-constantan) thermocouples cover the range of most spacecraft hardware, from cryogenic temperatures to a few hundred degrees Celsius. A system-level test article typically carries hundreds of TCs, and large observatories carry more. They measure structure, component case, and interface temperatures. TC locations are planned against the thermal model's node map so that every measurement can be compared directly with a prediction.
📡
RTDs and Cryogenic Sensors
Platinum resistance temperature detectors (PT100, PT1000) provide higher accuracy and stability than thermocouples. They are used at critical correlation nodes and wherever measurement uncertainty directly affects a margin assessment. At deep cryogenic temperatures, where platinum sensors lose sensitivity, programs use silicon diode and similar specialized cryogenic sensors instead.
⚡
Flight Thermistors
The spacecraft's own temperature sensors, which report through flight telemetry. During T-V testing they are compared with independent test sensors at the same locations, and they must agree within their stated tolerance. When they do not, the cause is as likely to be in the ground system as in the sensor: a wrong conversion curve, a channel mapped to the wrong mnemonic, or two sensors swapped in the telemetry database. Finding and fixing those errors before launch is one of the quieter but more valuable products of the test, because the operations team will rely on those readings for the life of the mission.
🔬
QCM and RGA
Quartz Crystal Microbalances (QCMs) monitor molecular contamination deposition rates throughout the test. Residual Gas Analyzers (RGAs) provide mass spectrometric analysis of the chamber gas composition, identifying specific outgassing species. Both are essential for characterizing the spacecraft's outgassing profile, identifying contaminating materials, and providing data for post-test contamination assessments on optical hardware.

A key data product of the T-V test is the temperature-time history for every instrumented point on the spacecraft. This dataset is the primary input to thermal model correlation and is archived as part of the permanent test record. Loss of this data — through data acquisition system failures, data labeling errors, or inadequate archiving protocols — is a serious test program failure that can invalidate the correlation effort and leave the thermal design without a verified model.

Instrumenting the Spacecraft Is Its Own Campaign

Installing several hundred test sensors on flight hardware is an I&T activity, not a footnote to the test. Each sensor needs an approved attachment method for the surface it goes on, because tapes and adhesives must be compatible with thermal coatings, optical surfaces, and contamination requirements, and must come off cleanly afterward. Lead wires have to be routed so they do not create heat paths, block radiators, or interfere with MLI closeout. Every channel must be traced from its physical location, through the chamber feedthrough, to its data system channel and display label, and the result controlled as a configuration-managed map. Each sensor is then verified in place before closeout, typically by warming it locally and confirming that the right channel responds.

A swapped or mislabeled channel discovered during correlation can cost far more than the hours it would have taken to verify it before the door closed. At worst, it can make some of the test data unusable. Removal deserves the same planning: a defined sequence, inspection of each attachment site, and accounting for every sensor and every piece of tape. Installation and removal both belong on the integrated schedule as real tasks with real durations.

11. Special Topics: Cryogenic Testing and Bake-Out

Cryogenic Testing

Science instruments with infrared or submillimeter detectors typically require operating temperatures below 100 K — in many cases below 10 K. The T-V test program for these instruments must be capable of achieving and sustaining these temperatures for the full duration of the test campaign. This requires cryogenic chamber configurations with liquid helium cooling systems, cryogenic radiative shrouds maintained at liquid nitrogen temperatures to provide a low-background radiant environment, and special attention to thermal isolation of the cold structure from the warmer spacecraft bus.

Cryogenic T-V tests are among the most expensive and time-consuming in the entire I&T program. Cooldown of a large cryogenic instrument to its operating temperature — through successive cooling stages using LN₂ pre-cooling, followed by liquid helium or mechanical cryocooler operation — can take days to weeks. Any interruption to the cooldown sequence (heater circuit failure, LN₂ supply interruption, facility power outage) may require full warm-up and restart, consuming weeks of schedule. Facility reliability and emergency backup systems are mission-critical infrastructure for cryogenic T-V campaigns.

Bake-Out During T-V

Some outgassing happens as soon as the chamber is pumped down, but a bake-out is a deliberate hot phase. The spacecraft, or selected hardware, is held at an elevated temperature in vacuum while the chamber's cold surfaces and contamination monitors capture what comes off. It ends when a specified criterion is met, typically a deposition rate measured by a QCM falling below a required value. Many programs place the bake-out as the final hot phase of the campaign, just before warm-up and repressurization, so the hardware leaves the chamber in its cleanest condition.

For the I&T lead, the bake-out has the same schedule character as a thermal balance plateau: it ends when the hardware meets the criterion, not when the timeline says it should. Contamination engineering owns the criterion. The I&T lead needs a realistic duration estimate, an agreed position on what happens if the rate is slow to come down, and assurance that the bake-out temperature stays within every component's limits. The hottest bake-out temperature the outgassing would benefit from is not always one every unit can tolerate.

⚙ JWST and Hurricane Harvey: When the Facility Is the Risk

The cryogenic vacuum test of JWST's telescope and science instrument module (OTIS) in Chamber A at Johnson Space Center ran for 93 days in 2017. Liquid-nitrogen and cold gaseous-helium shrouds drew the heat out of the hardware, and the cooldown alone took about a month. An interruption that forced a warm-up would have meant warming the hardware up and then spending another month cooling it back down.

In late August, Hurricane Harvey struck Houston just as the telescope reached stable cryogenic temperatures. Johnson Space Center was closed to regular employees for about nine days, but the test team stayed, working extended shifts, and some slept on site. Optical testing was curtailed for a time, but the test itself was never forced to warm up. Flooded roads cut off liquid nitrogen deliveries for about three days against a five-day reserve, and the team had to arrange an alternate supplier. The center never lost commercial power, which would have stopped the helium refrigerator and forced the shroud to warm.

The lesson is the one in Section 8: facility failures are spacecraft risks, and the protection against them is built before the test starts. Backup power, cryogen supply contingencies, a staffing plan that holds up when people cannot get to the site, and a defined spacecraft response to each credible facility failure are all part of the test design.

12. Limitations of Ground Thermal Testing

Thermal-vacuum testing, however thorough, cannot perfectly replicate the on-orbit thermal environment. Understanding the limitations of ground testing is essential for correctly interpreting test results and for identifying where analysis must supplement testing in the overall verification argument.

  • Gravity effects on fluids and enclosed gas: High vacuum eliminates convection around the spacecraft, but not inside it. Wherever the spacecraft contains fluid or trapped gas, buoyancy-driven flow on the ground moves heat in ways it will not in orbit. That includes heat pipes and fluid loops, propellant and pressurant, battery cells, and sealed enclosures. Ground testing verifies thermal conductance under gravity, and analysis has to bridge the difference to microgravity.
  • Heat pipe orientation: On the ground, a heat pipe works as designed only when it is level or oriented so gravity assists the liquid return. If it is tilted the wrong way, it can dry out and stop carrying heat, which looks like a thermal design failure but is a test configuration error. Heat pipe orientation therefore constrains how the spacecraft sits in the chamber, how the test fixture is designed, and how precisely it must be leveled. The thermal team defines the requirement; the I&T lead has to build a configuration that meets it and verify it before the door closes.
  • Solar simulator fidelity: Solar simulators do not perfectly replicate the solar spectrum. Differences in spectral distribution affect the absorptance of thermal control coatings and solar cells. The error introduced by solar simulator spectral mismatch must be quantified and accounted for in the thermal model correlation process.
  • Background radiation environment: LN₂-cooled shrouds provide a background of roughly 77 K rather than the ~3 K of deep space. For hardware at normal spacecraft temperatures, the difference in radiated heat is small. For cryogenic hardware it is not, which is why cryogenic tests need colder, helium-cooled shrouds (Section 11). The chamber also does not reproduce Earth albedo and Earth infrared emission in their real magnitude and angular distribution. Test heaters and boundary conditions are designed to produce equivalent temperatures, but the equivalence is never perfect and has to be accounted for in correlation.
  • Long-term on-orbit degradation: Thermal control coating optical properties (solar absorptance α, infrared emittance ε) degrade with radiation exposure over the mission lifetime. The thermal design must account for end-of-life coating properties, which are worse than beginning-of-life values. Ground T-V testing uses beginning-of-life (or specified end-of-life) coating properties; the actual on-orbit performance at end of life can only be bounded by analysis using the tested and correlated model with degraded coating properties applied.
  • Test configuration is not flight configuration: In the chamber, deployable appendages are usually stowed, large solar arrays may be absent or simulated, and MGSE, test cabling, and test sensors add heat paths and block views to cold space. Some thermal blankets may be test-specific or not yet in final flight condition. Each difference must be identified before the test, accounted for in the thermal model of the test configuration, and tracked to closure afterward. That includes every item installed for the test that must be removed before flight. The configuration differences list is an I&T product, and it belongs under configuration control.
✓ A Note on On-Orbit Commissioning

The limitations of ground thermal testing make on-orbit commissioning — the period of spacecraft checkout and characterization following launch — an essential complement to the ground test program. During commissioning, the spacecraft is operated through its full range of thermal conditions in the actual flight environment. Temperature telemetry is compared to the predictions of the correlated thermal model. Discrepancies that were not resolvable from ground data alone become resolvable once the spacecraft is exposed to its actual orbital environment.

The first thermal eclipse transition, the first hot-case orbital geometry, and the first extended cold-soak period in safe mode are among the most carefully monitored events in any spacecraft's early mission. The engineering team that worked the T-V test program will be watching the telemetry in the mission operations center, comparing what they see to what the model predicts, and ready to respond if the two diverge.

Summary

Thermal-vacuum testing is the most sustained and logistically complex test campaign in the spacecraft I&T program. It combines high vacuum with the full range of spacecraft operating temperatures, and it exercises the thermal design and the functional capability of the spacecraft at the same time, for weeks. Done correctly, it produces four things that cannot be obtained any other way: a correlated thermal model, a verified thermal design, a functional performance record across the operating temperature range, and a workmanship screen of the integrated vehicle in a representative environment.

The technical content of the test belongs to the thermal, contamination, and subsystem engineers. The I&T lead's contribution is making it all happen inside one campaign. That means folding every objective and its owner's time estimate into one integrated plan before the test, building a chamber configuration and an instrumentation set that can deliver them, and getting every signal through the chamber wall. It also means staffing the campaign around the clock in a way people can sustain for weeks, preparing for facility failures and anomalies before the door closes, and making sure that when schedule pressure arrives, the options and their risks reach the people who own those decisions and the outcome is carried out deliberately. Every unexpected temperature, every heater that does not respond, and every outgassing event is an engineering finding to be understood before flight, not a nuisance to be worked around.

The next article addresses electromagnetic compatibility testing — the verification that the spacecraft's electronic systems can operate simultaneously without interfering with each other, and the test campaign that only a fully integrated spacecraft can reveal.

Key Terms Used in This Article

TermDefinition
AFT (Allowable Flight Temperature)A term used by JPL and many other organizations for the temperature range a component is allowed to reach in flight, with defined margins to test temperatures. GSFC practice frames the same margins relative to predicted flight temperatures instead. Whatever terminology a program uses, it must be applied consistently in every procedure and redline table.
AlbedoSunlight reflected from the Earth (or another body) onto the spacecraft. One of the three external heat inputs for Earth-orbiting spacecraft, together with direct sunlight and Earth infrared emission.
Bake-OutA deliberate hot phase in vacuum that drives volatile compounds out of hardware while chamber cold surfaces capture them. It ends when a contamination criterion is met, typically a QCM deposition rate below a specified value. It is often scheduled as the final hot phase of a T-V campaign.
Cold CaseThe combination of conditions that drives component temperatures lowest: minimum solar flux, maximum eclipse, beginning-of-life coating properties, and minimum internal power dissipation. It is the limiting case for heater circuits, batteries, and propulsion components.
Cold Start (Cold Turn-On)A demonstration in which unpowered hardware is driven toward its survival limits and then powered on to show that it starts and operates correctly from that condition. It is one of the highest-risk events in a T-V campaign.
Corona (Critical Pressure)Electrical breakdown that can occur in high-voltage equipment within a range of intermediate pressures. High-voltage equipment is kept off while the chamber passes through this range during pumpdown and repressurization.
Heat PipeA sealed passive device that transports heat using an evaporating and condensing working fluid. On the ground, gravity affects its operation, so heat pipe orientation constrains how a spacecraft is positioned and leveled in the chamber.
Hot CaseThe combination of conditions that drives component temperatures highest: maximum solar flux, minimum eclipse, end-of-life coating properties, and maximum internal power dissipation. It verifies the thermal design's heat rejection capacity.
MLI (Multilayer Insulation)A thermal blanket consisting of multiple layers of aluminized Mylar or Kapton with low-emittance surfaces and low-conductivity spacers, used to insulate spacecraft surfaces from the radiative environment. The primary passive thermal control material on most spacecraft.
Predicted Flight TemperaturesThe hottest and coldest temperatures each component is expected to reach over the mission, as calculated by the thermal mathematical model with its required uncertainty margin. The baseline from which test temperatures are set.
QCM (Quartz Crystal Microbalance)A contamination monitoring instrument that measures molecular deposition rates using changes in the resonant frequency of a quartz crystal. Used inside T-V chambers to characterize outgassing and to judge when a bake-out is complete.
RepressurizationThe controlled return of the chamber to ambient pressure at the end of a T-V test, normally by backfilling with dry nitrogen after the hardware has been warmed above the dew point. A contamination event that requires its own agreed procedure.
RGA (Residual Gas Analyzer)A mass spectrometer used inside a T-V chamber to identify the species present in the residual gas atmosphere, characterizing the outgassing products of spacecraft materials.
Solar ConstantThe solar irradiance at Earth's mean distance from the Sun (1 AU), approximately 1,361 W/m². It varies over the year from roughly 1,322 W/m² near aphelion to 1,414 W/m² near perihelion.
Stability CriterionThe test-plan definition of steady state for a thermal balance case, expressed as a maximum rate of temperature change at specified locations over a specified period. It determines how long a balance plateau lasts.
Survival LimitsThe temperature range within which hardware will not be permanently damaged, though it may not function correctly. Hardware must survive these limits non-operating, with power off.
Test-Limiting ComponentThe component that reaches its test temperature limit first as the spacecraft is driven toward a hot or cold plateau, preventing the rest of the vehicle from reaching its own targets without local test heating or cooling.
Thermal Balance Test (TBT)A T-V test phase that establishes controlled steady-state thermal conditions corresponding to defined mission environments (hot and cold cases) for comparison with thermal mathematical model predictions.
Thermal Mathematical Model (TMM)A computational model of the spacecraft's thermal design that predicts component temperatures under defined environmental inputs. Must be correlated to T-V test data before it can be used with confidence for on-orbit predictions.
Thermal Ramp RateThe rate of temperature change during thermal transitions, in °C per minute. Controlled directly at the component level. At system level it is set mostly by the spacecraft's thermal mass and the chamber shrouds, and the governing constraint is usually a maximum rate for sensitive hardware.
Thermal Vacuum CyclingRepeated cycling between hot and cold test temperatures inside a vacuum chamber, performed at both component and system levels. It combines workmanship screening with functional verification at temperature, and its minimum cycle counts are set by the governing standard.
